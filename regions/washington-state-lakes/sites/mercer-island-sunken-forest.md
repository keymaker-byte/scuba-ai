# Mercer Island Sunken Forest

A drowned stand of old-growth trees off Clarke Beach Park on the southeast shore of Mercer Island, carried into Lake Washington by landslides during a large Seattle Fault earthquake about a thousand years ago and preserved by the cold, low-oxygen water. The trunks stand and lie across the slope from about 12 to 18 m, a short swim from a park pier, with the trees themselves the whole attraction. The water is dark and murky at depth, the bottom is fine silt, and old monofilament fishing line hangs from the logs, so entanglement in poor visibility is the headline hazard.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Two ways in from the waterfront: down the stairs at the shoreline, whose last step is a big drop into the water and awkward in doubles, or down the ladder off the pier. The pier ladder is the easier exit, and its rungs are slick. Swim on the surface about 30 m, roughly the length of the pier, and descend.
* **Parking.** A free lot off East Mercer Way at SE 77th Place, at the top of the park, open with no pass.
* **Access.** A steep paved trail of about 140 m drops roughly 27 m from the lot to the waterfront, a long carry with doubles. The waterfront is unguarded. The enclosed swim area, its pier and the sheet-pile wall have reached the end of their service life and are slated for replacement under the city's park plan, with no construction date set, so check the park's status before driving out. The drowned trees belong to the State of Washington under the Department of Natural Resources and are protected from salvage; take nothing.
* **Facilities.** A fishing pier, a swim pier, the enclosed swim area, picnic tables near the water that serve as a staging area, and a restroom building behind the swim area, open in summer.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The pier.** The descent point: drop near the end of the pier, where the bottom falls from the shallow shelf onto the slope.
* **The slope.** Runs east-southeast from the entry, about 116°, from 5 m at the foot of the shore to 12 m within about 50 m and 18 m within about 75 m. A compass bearing out and back is the only reliable way to hold a line in the dark.
* **The forest.** Dead trunks, mostly bare, stand and lie across the slope between about 12 and 18 m. A fallen trunk running down the slope along the bottom at around 16 m is a natural line to follow out and back.
* **Beyond the trees.** The bottom flattens past the forest, rises slightly over a low mound about 100 to 130 m out, then keeps descending gently past 23 m, beyond the usual dive.

## Current

The water at depth is still; wind moves the surface alone.

* **Type.** None.
* **Surface drift.** A sustained wind puts a light drift on a diver waiting at the surface.

The entry sits on the island's southeast shore, facing down the south end of the lake toward Renton, with the island close behind it to the north and west.

* **Short fetch.** South-southeast through south-southwest (SSE-SSW), onshore over 1.1 to 3.6 km of open water. A sustained southerly runs chop straight onto the stairs and the pier ladder, which makes both the entry and the exit rough.
* **Fine.** Southwest clockwise through southeast (SW-SE): offshore from northwest through east-northeast, where the island blocks the wind, and under 1 km of open water from every other direction.

## Visibility

* **At depth.** Low and murky, and dark among the trees even in daylight; a primary light is essential. Fine silt covers the bottom and a careless fin kick drops the visibility to nothing in seconds.
* **Through the season.** Visibility figures by season are not established.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Cold year round below the thermocline, colder than the calm surface suggests. Figures at the forest's depth are not established. Dive it in a drysuit.
* **Surface layer.** Warms through summer while the lake is stratified, from late March to early November, then cools as the lake mixes in late fall.

## Marine life

* **The trees.** The forest is a geological attraction: bare, hard, mineral-preserved trunks of an old-growth stand, which now shelter the lake's animals. A salvage barge illegally cut and lifted trees from the forest off the island's southeast side in the early 1990s, and dozens of cut stumps and tree parts lie among the standing trunks.
* **Crayfish and fish.** Signal crayfish live in the lake and turn up among the logs and debris. Yellow perch and smallmouth and largemouth bass are among the lake's common fish.

## Hazards

* **Monofilament.** Old fishing line hangs from the logs, some of it stretched taut in mid-water where it is hardest to see. Carry two cutting tools, within reach of either hand.
* **Darkness and disorientation.** The water is dark at the forest's depth, and trunks loom out of the dark at close range; with no current to read, a diver's own drift is hard to judge. Hold a compass bearing, stay close to a reliable buddy, and keep the dive inside a known line.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine and stirs instantly. Fin carefully, stay off the bottom, and turn back on a planned bearing as soon as visibility goes.
* **Boat traffic.** Motorboats run the lake through summer. Mercer Island Police patrol the lake. Fly a dive flag from the moment the team enters the water, surface near the shore on the swim back, and listen for engines before ascending.
* **The entry.** The drop off the last stair and the slick pier ladder are the likeliest places to get hurt, especially in heavy gear.
* **Cold.** The water below the thermocline stays cold even in midsummer, and the dark water and slow pace of the dive let a chill build unnoticed.
