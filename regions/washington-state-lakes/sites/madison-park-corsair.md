# Madison Park Corsair

The broken wreck of a US Navy FG-1D Corsair fighter on the bed of Lake Washington, a shore dive from the swimming beach at Madison Park in Seattle. The plane, BuNo 87833, flown by squadron VA-891, collided with another of the squadron's Corsairs from Naval Air Station Seattle at Sand Point during a formation exercise on 29 July 1950; its pilot bailed out and parachuted into the lake, and the plane struck the water hard a short way off this shore and broke apart. Wings, fuselage, landing gear and ammunition boxes now lie scattered over about 90 m of silty bottom at 21 to 27 m, in dark water below the summer thermocline. The headline hazards are boat traffic on the open lake over the wreck and the dark, cold water at its depth.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk down the lawn to the swimming beach and enter from the sand at its south end, beside the tall apartment building. The water is about 1.5 m deep 20 m out from the entry. The dive area is 163 m out, east-southeast, 114°, over 24.2 m of water.
* **Parking.** Street parking around the park, at the top of the lawn above the beach.
* **Access.** A Seattle city park, open 4 a.m. to 11:30 p.m. From mid to late June through Labor Day the beach is a lifeguarded designated swimming area, and city code requires written permission from the Chief of Police, through the Seattle Police Harbor Patrol, to dive within 91 m of a patrolled designated swimming area. City code bars powered boats from within 91 m of a swimming beach, and also requires a diver flag, and the blue and white Alpha flag does not satisfy it.
* **Facilities.** A bathhouse with restrooms, benches, a play area and tennis courts. In summer a swimming raft with diving boards sits off the beach.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The beach.** A grassy park slopes down to a beach about 120 m long, with cement steps along its north part and a short stretch of sand at its south end, beside the tall apartment building.
* **The slope.** Straight out east from the entry the bottom slopes steadily: about 4 m deep 50 m from the entry, 8 m at 80 m, 13 m at 100 m and 17 m at 120 m, reaching about 21 m at 140 m and 25 m at 160 m.
* **Finding the wreck.** The debris lies to the right of straight out from the beach. Swim out from the south end of the beach, near the apartment building, to about 21 m, then turn right, south along the slope, to the debris. Heading east-southeast, 114°, from the entry, the bottom reads about 20 m at 145 m out, 24.2 m at the dive area 163 m out, and 27 m at 185 m.
* **The wreck.** A tangle of pieces scattered over about 90 m: broken wings in one place, the crumpled fuselage in another, with the landing gear and ammunition boxes among them. Some parts were raised in 1983.
* **Beyond.** Past the wreck the bottom keeps sloping east, through about 31 m at 225 m out and 34 m at 285 m.

## Current

* **Type.** None: the lake holds still at depth, and wind drives only a surface drift.

## Wind

|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | northeast, 50° |

The swim out runs 63° off the entry shore's facing, to the right of straight out from the beach.

* **Bad.** North-northeast through northeast (NNE-NE), onshore over 5.3 to 6.6 km of open water. A sustained wind from here builds chop straight onto the beach, which makes the entry and the long surface swim back rough.
* **Short fetch.** North (N), and east-northeast through east-southeast (ENE-ESE), onshore over 2.0 to 2.7 km. A sustained wind from here puts a chop on the beach, enough to make the exit uncomfortable in a stiff breeze.
* **Mixed.** Southeast through south-southeast (SE-SSE), along the shore over 5.0 to 7.1 km. A sustained wind from here runs a chop past the beach and across the surface swim.
* **Fine.** South clockwise through north-northwest (S-NNW): offshore from south through west-northwest, with the park and the city behind the beach, and cross-shore from northwest and north-northwest over 210 m or less.

## Visibility

* **Darkness at depth.** The water at the wreck's depth, below the summer thermocline, is dark. Carry a primary light.
* **Silt.** The bottom is soft silt, which clouds the water around a diver who stirs it.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** In late summer the water below the thermocline runs about 10 to 11 °C at the wreck's depth, and the lake runs cold top to bottom through winter. Drysuit protocol applies year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms to about 23 °C in late summer, over a thermocline running from about 11 to 21 m, through which the temperature falls about 1 °C a metre.

## Marine life

* **On the wreck.** Sculpin and signal crayfish shelter among the wreckage.

## Hazards

* **Boat traffic.** The wreck lies out in the open lake, in the busy boat traffic running to and from the channel under the SR 520 bridge in boating season. Seattle's 7 knot (13 km/h) speed limit holds within 91 m of the shore, and in the lifeguard season powered boats are barred from within 91 m of the swimming beach; the wreck lies beyond both. Surface with a diver flag or marker buoy deployed, listen for engines before ascending, and make the ascent up the slope toward the beach. Winter brings far less traffic.
* **The swimming area.** From mid to late June through Labor Day swimmers crowd the beach and the water off it, and diving within 91 m of the patrolled swimming area needs written permission from the Chief of Police, through the Seattle Police Harbor Patrol.
* **Darkness and depth.** The wreck lies at 21 to 27 m in dark water, at a depth that takes a careful eye on the computer and the gas. Plan a turn depth and turn pressure before leaving the beach.
* **The long swim.** The dive area is 163 m from the entry, a long surface swim each way in a drysuit, and a long underwater return on the gas left at the wreck.
* **Sharp metal.** The wreck is torn and twisted metal with sharp edges.
* **Military wreck.** The plane remains property of the US Navy under the Sunken Military Craft Act. Look, photograph, and leave every part where it lies.
* **Weighting.** Fresh water gives less lift than salt water, and a diver weighted for the sea sinks fast down the slope. Drop weight for fresh water and check buoyancy in the shallows before heading out.
* **Cold at depth.** The water below the thermocline stays about 10 to 11 °C even in late summer, cold enough that hypothermia builds faster than the warm surface suggests.
