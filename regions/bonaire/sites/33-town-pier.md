# 33. Town Pier

Kralendijk's town pier, a working cargo and cruise pier running out from the waterfront opposite Wilhelmina Park, whose pilings carry decades of growth: orange cup corals and the greatest variety of sponge size and shape anywhere on Bonaire. It is a classic night dive, a shallow, slow macro dive among the pilings for seahorses, frogfish, morays and crabs, with tarpon hunting in the pier lights. It is also the one marine park site that needs the Harbourmaster's permission and a local guide, and permits have rarely been issued in recent years. The headline hazard is the working harbour itself: tugs and ships manoeuvring at the pier.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the pier, where the guide leads the group down to the water, a drop of about 1.5 m from the deck, and back out the same way. The shore just south of the pier, a small sandy beach beside concrete shore protection reached by a narrow path from the boulevard, is a second way in; waves can break on the shore protection, so time the entry for a lull. The dive area lies 55 m northwest of the entry, off the head of the pier.
* **Parking.** On the waterfront at the foot of the pier, as arranged with the guide, or along Kaya Grandi.
* **Access.** Diving at the pier needs written permission from the Harbourmaster and a local divemaster leading every group, four divers at most per guide. The dive operator submits each diver's paperwork, a passport copy included, at least 24 hours ahead, and the guide checks the group in on the night, with a guide fee charged on top of a standard dive package. Permits have rarely been issued in recent years, and the site is often closed to diving; confirm its status with a dive operator before planning around it. Diving stops whenever a cruise ship, cargo ship or tug is at the pier. The Nature Fee e-tag is required.
* **Facilities.** None. The pier is a working commercial pier, floodlit at night.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The inner pilings.** From the entry the dive works in under the pier toward the shore, where the bottom shoals to 3 to 5 m and the pilings stand close together, so the group goes single file.
* **The outer pilings.** Back out along the pier the pilings spread apart over a bottom of 4 to 6 m, with sponges, orange cup corals and small corals covering every piling. The dive moves slowly out along them toward the head of the pier.
* **The bottom.** Sand and coral rubble strewn with tyres, concrete blocks and harbour debris, much of it now grown over and sheltering eels and crabs.
* **The pier head.** The pier ends in a short L. Off its head the bottom drops to 8 to 11 m, then on past 20 m into the harbour.
* **The return.** From the pier head back along the pilings to the entry, 55 m southeast.

* **Drivers.** The east to southeast trade winds. The current can sit still for days, then run harder when the trades strengthen or shift. Tugs and ships manoeuvring at the pier throw a strong local current through the pilings from their propellers.
* **Character.** The harbour is sheltered and the current under the pier is usually mild. It strengthens in bad weather.

## Wind

|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | west, 261° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | west, 262° |

The swim out runs 55 m northwest from the entry along the pier to the dive area, 48° off the shore's facing.

* **Bad.** Southwest through west-southwest (SW-WSW), onshore over more than 30 km of open sea through the gap south of Klein Bonaire, and northwest (NW), onshore over 10.7 km down the coast. Wind from these quarters raises waves on the shore protection and a surge through the pilings.
* **Short fetch.** South-southwest (SSW), onshore over 3.5 km, and west through west-northwest (W-WNW), onshore over 1.7 to 1.8 km from Klein Bonaire, each enough to build a chop at the entry.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through south (NNW-S): offshore from north-northeast through southeast, off the land, and cross-shore over 380 m to 1.6 km from the north-northwest, north, south-southeast and south. The prevailing east to southeast trades blow off the land and leave the harbour calm at their usual strength.

## Visibility

* **Overall.** 15 to 25 m on a typical day in the sheltered harbour water.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 26 to 29 °C year round, coolest around February and March, warmest around August through October. The water is evenly mixed through these shallow depths. A dive skin, shorty or 3 mm wetsuit suits.

## Marine life

* **The pilings.** Decades of growth: the greatest variety of sponge size and shape found anywhere on Bonaire, orange cup corals that open into bright orange polyps at night, encrusting sponges and small corals.
* **Macro.** Seahorses and frogfish are found among the growth on the pilings. Arrow crabs, decorator crabs, shrimp and brittle stars cover the pilings, with basket stars unfurling at night. Many species are found here that are seldom seen elsewhere on the island.
* **Eels.** Green, goldentail, chain and sharptail morays live under the pier, among the concrete blocks and in the old tyres, and leave their holes to hunt at night.
* **Fish.** Large schools of sergeant majors gather around the pilings by day, with trumpetfish hanging among them and French angelfish and parrotfish on the overgrown concrete blocks. Spotted drum shelter under the pier.
* **Night.** Tarpon hunt in the pier floodlights and divers' lights, parrotfish sleep among the pilings, octopus and lobsters forage, and turtles rest under the pier.

## Hazards

* **Working harbour.** Tugs berth at the pier and ships use it day and night. A tug starting its engines or manoeuvring puts loud propeller noise and a strong current through the pilings; leave the water at once with the guide when it does. Dive only with the guide's confirmation that no movements are scheduled.
* **Close pilings.** The inner pilings stand close together, and the group moves single file among them. Fine buoyancy control keeps fins and gauges off the growth and off other divers; a light marking the guide helps hold the group together at night.
* **Debris.** Tyres, concrete blocks, rough concrete and sharp metal lie on the bottom; keep hands and hoses clear.
* **Boat traffic.** Water taxis, dive boats and jet skis use the harbour. Stay down under the pier until back at the entry, and carry a surface marker buoy.
* **Waves at the entry.** Wind from southwest through west-southwest (SW-WSW) or northwest (NW) puts waves on the shore protection, and from the south-southwest (SSW) or west through west-northwest (W-WNW) a chop; choose another site when any of these is blowing.
