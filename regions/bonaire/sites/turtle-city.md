# Turtle City

A broad, shallow reef plateau of sea fans and soft coral on Bonaire's east coast, south of the mouth of Lac Bay, where green turtles rest by the dozen in the reef crevices and on the sand between the coral. The plateau runs at about 4 to 10 m and tips over at about 10 m onto the outer reef slope. The site lies outside the marine park's numbered dive sites and is dived by boat with an east coast operator as a guided drift. It lies on the windward coast, and waves and surge across the shallow plateau decide on the day whether it can be dived.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** A backward roll from the operator's rigid inflatable, with re-entry by ladder through a removable section of the boat's side. Boats leave from the fishermen's pier at Sorobon and cross Lac Bay out to the site in about five minutes.
* **Parking.** At the fishermen's pier at Sorobon, at the end of the road past the Jibe City windsurf shops.
* **Access.** Dived as a guided drift with Bonaire East Coast Diving, the specialist east coast boat operator, on small groups booked in advance. Divers bring all their own gear, weights and a dive computer, with tanks supplied, and are expected to hold steady buoyancy and carry enough gas for a dive of about an hour. The site has no mooring, and the boat follows the group on the drift. The reef lies beyond a long, wave-swept stretch of shallows with no practical shore entry for a diver in gear, so the site is reached by boat. A valid STINAPA nature fee is required.
* **Facilities.** None at the site.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The plateau.** A wide, flat reef plateau at about 4 m near shore deepens gently seaward to 8 to 10 m at the reef edge, about 400 m offshore. Large fields of sea fans and soft coral cover it, waving in the surge, broken by small sandy patches.
* **The reef.** Toward the reef edge the coral rises into rocks and heads with cavities and crevices between them, where turtles wedge themselves in to rest.
* **The cleaning station.** A turtle cleaning station at about 6 m, where wrasses pick the turtles clean and turtles wait their turn.
* **The reef edge and slope.** At about 10 m the plateau tips over onto the outer reef slope, which drops away into the open ocean past the depths dived.
* **Dive pattern.** Descend on the plateau or on the upper slope, sometimes as deep as 20 m, and drift with the guide along the plateau and the top of the reef edge, then make a free ascent in open water behind the guide's surface marker to a boat pickup. In calm conditions the dive can run as one long drift together with a sand-floored hole in the plateau about 230 m to the north-northwest.

* **Drivers.** The trade winds, blowing from the east-northeast through east-southeast. The drift strengthens when the trades pick up or shift.
* **Drift.** The site is dived as a guided drift with the boat following, usually northward along the reef. On most days the current over the plateau runs mild to minimal, and it can change in strength and direction during a dive.
* **Surge.** Waves breaking over the shallow plateau put a strong surge on it, strongest in the shallows among the sea fans.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Dive area shore facing** | east-northeast, 78° |

The nearest shore lies 400 m to the west-southwest, 246°, and the plateau opens onto more than 30 km of ocean from east-northeast through south-southeast.

* **Bad.** East-northeast through southeast (ENE-SE), onshore over more than 30 km of open ocean. The trades blow from this quarter most of the year, building waves that break across the shallow plateau and make the boat entry and exit and the descent rough.
* **Short fetch.** North-northeast through northeast (NNE-NE), onshore over 1.5 to 2.1 km, enough to build a chop over the plateau.
* **Mixed.** South-southeast (SSE), cross-shore over more than 30 km, running a swell along the plateau.
* **Fine.** South clockwise through north: offshore from south-southwest through northwest, off the land over 400 m to 2 km, and cross-shore over 2.2 to 2.9 km from the south, north-northwest and north.

## Visibility

* **Overall.** Commonly 15 to 25 m, ranging from about 10 to 30 m. Waves and surge stirring the shallow plateau bring it down, and calm early mornings run clearest.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 26 to 29 °C year round, coolest around February and March, warmest around August through October. The water stays evenly mixed through these shallow depths. A wetsuit or dive skin is standard.

## Marine life

* **Turtles.** Green turtles in great numbers, commonly 25 to 50 on a dive and at times more than 75, many of them large. They feed on the seagrass inside Lac Bay and come out to the reef to rest, wedged into the crevices, lying on the sand, and visiting the cleaning station, often resting as shallow as 5 m. Few divers visit, so the turtles are shy: approach from the side, leaving them a clear way out, keep at least a metre away, and never chase one. A few hawksbill turtles are seen among them.
* **Rays.** Spotted eagle rays passing along the reef edge and over the slope, and southern stingrays on the sand.
* **Reef fish.** Green morays and lobsters in the reef crevices, grunts, snapper and queen triggerfish among the coral, flamingo tongues on the soft coral, and barracuda and tarpon around the plateau. Lionfish are present.
* **Occasional.** Nurse sharks, with larger pelagic fish passing along the reef edge.

## Hazards

* **East coast exposure.** The site faces the open ocean on the windward coast. Swell of about 1 m is common on the surface, making the descent, the wait after surfacing and the climb back aboard demanding, and conditions can build quickly through the day as the trades strengthen. Keep the regulator in until back on the boat.
* **Seasickness.** The boat ride and the wait at the surface in the swell bring on seasickness in many divers.
* **Drift.** The current can change in strength and direction during the dive. Stay with the guide, carry a surface marker buoy and whistle, and surface to the boat as briefed.
* **Surge.** The strong surge over the shallow plateau can push a diver into the coral; hold buoyancy clear of the reef.
* **Remote site.** Help is a boat ride and a drive from Kralendijk, so rescue and evacuation take longer than on the west coast.
