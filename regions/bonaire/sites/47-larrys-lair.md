# 47. Larry's Lair

Larry's Lair is a double reef shore site on EEG Boulevard, about 780 m north of Salt Pier on Bonaire's southwest coast. A short step down off the ironshore leads onto a wide, shallow terrace that a 165 m swim crosses to the top of the first reef at about 10.4 m. The first reef slopes down to sand from about 30 m, and a second reef rises beyond the sand channel from about 28 m. No mooring buoy marks the site, so the swim out, the reef and the way home all run on compass and natural landmarks; the headline hazards are navigation and the depth of the sand between the reefs.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk about 30 m west from the road to the ironshore. The best way in lies a little north of the southernmost yellow stone, where the ironshore slopes into the water; elsewhere it ends in a short drop. The rock is sharp, slick in spots and carries small urchins, so wear boots with sturdy soles and pick the way in and out with care. Past the edge, the bottom is firm underfoot at about 3.5 m. Swim 165 m west, close to straight out from the shore, across the terrace to the top of the first reef. Exit at the same slope.
* **Parking.** The roadside shoulder of EEG Boulevard, free. The shoulder is loose sand; park on firm ground and keep vehicles off the ironshore.
* **Access.** Open at all hours. The STINAPA Nature Fee e-tag is the only fee.
* **Facilities.** None at the site, and very little shade.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The entry.** Take a compass bearing and note the shore around the slope before swimming out: no buoy marks the site from the surface or below, and the ironshore either side of the slope is hard to climb out on.
* **The terrace.** Sand, rubble and hard pan with patches of elkhorn and staghorn and scattered live coral heads, about 3.5 to 5 m deep for the first 130 m, then dropping to the reef top over the last 35 m. It is worth swimming underwater, and it serves as the safety stop on the way back.
* **The first reef.** The dive area, 165 m west of the entry, sits on the reef top at about 10.4 m. The reef carries on along the coast north and south at the same depth and slopes away seaward to sand from about 30 m. Along the reef, head north or south into the current.
* **The northern spur.** Well north along the first reef, a broad spur reaches out toward the second reef from about 23 m and deeper.
* **The sand channel.** The sand between the reefs keeps deepening past 30 m to about 35 to 43 m, out of sight of the first reef on most days.
* **The second reef.** Rises beyond the channel from about 28 m. Reaching it means a compass heading across the sand and back.
* **The return.** Come back up the first reef onto the terrace and head east, 165 m to the slope in the ironshore.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | west, 260° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | west, 260° |

The swim out runs 165 m west from the entry, 4° off the shore's facing, close to straight out.

* **Bad.** South-southwest through northwest (SSW-NW), onshore over more than 30 km of open sea. Wind from this quarter drives waves onto the ironshore, stirs up the shallows and puts surge on the entry and exit.
* **Mixed.** South (S), cross-shore over more than 30 km, running a swell along the shore past the entry.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through south-southeast (NNW-SSE): offshore from north-northeast through southeast, off the land, and cross-shore over 820 m to 2.5 km from the north-northwest, north and south-southeast. The prevailing east to southeast trades blow off the land and leave the entry calm at their usual strength.

## Visibility

* **Overall.** Typically 15 to 30 m on the reef, best on calm mornings.
* **The shallows.** The terrace can run hazier than the reef, more so when wind or swell from south-southwest through northwest (SSW-NW) breaks on the shore.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 26 to 29 °C year round, coolest around February and March, warmest around August through October. A dive skin, shorty or 3 mm wetsuit suits.

## Marine life

* **Terrace.** Rays and turtles on the sandy shallows, Caribbean reef squid over the terrace, snake eels in the sand, and spotted eagle rays at times on the swim out.
* **First reef.** Large numbers of grunts, many butterflyfish, angelfish, parrotfish, trumpetfish and puffers among sponges, elephant ear sponges included. Octopus, frogfish and squirrelfish in the coral.
* **Sand and second reef.** Stingrays and garden eels on the sand between the reefs. Barracuda, mackerel and tarpon along the deeper reef.
* **Coral.** Hard coral and sponges on the reefs, with elkhorn and staghorn in the shallows. Many older coral formations on the first reef are dead, and hard coral cover along the whole leeward coast, elkhorn and staghorn included, has fallen sharply since 2023 from bleaching and disease.
* **Lionfish.** Invasive lionfish turn up on the reef.

## Hazards

* **Navigation.** No mooring buoy marks the site, from the surface or below. Hold a compass bearing out and back, and when unsure of the way home, climb the first reef onto the terrace and head east before surfacing.
* **Depth.** The sand between the reefs lies at about 35 to 43 m, past EAN32's 34 m maximum operating depth at a ppO2 of 1.4. Cross to the second reef only on a planned depth of 30 m or shallower, swimming above the sand, and keep within the no-decompression limit, which is short at that depth.
* **The entry and exit.** Sharp, slippery ironshore, small urchins and fire coral at the water's edge, and live coral heads in the shallows that are hard to avoid. Missing the slope on the way back means climbing out over a short drop in the ironshore; hold the bearing back to the slope across the terrace.
* **Gas.** The 165 m swim each way, plus any crossing to the second reef, uses a large share of a cylinder. Turn on gas with the swim home over the terrace still to come.
* **Surge and swell.** Wind or swell from south-southwest through northwest (SSW-NW) breaks on the ironshore and makes the entry and exit rough; choose another site when any of these blows fresh or a swell is running.
* **Boats.** Local fishing boats work this stretch of coast, and nothing on the surface marks divers below. Ascend with a surface marker buoy.
