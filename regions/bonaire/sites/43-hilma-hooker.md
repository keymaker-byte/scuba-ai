# 43. Hilma Hooker

Bonaire's best known wreck, a 72 m cargo ship lying on her starboard side on the sand channel between the inner and outer reefs off EEG Boulevard, about 6 km south of Kralendijk. Launched in the Netherlands in 1951 as the Midsland, she sailed under five more names before Colombian owners renamed her Hilma Hooker in 1979. In 1984 she put into Kralendijk with engine trouble, and a search found about 11,000 kg of marijuana behind a false bulkhead; held as evidence while no owner came forward, she was towed to an anchorage here on 7 September 1984 and on 12 September took water through her lower portholes, rolled onto her starboard side and sank within two minutes. A swim from shore crosses a shallow terrace to three buoys over the wreck, which runs from about 18 m on her uppermost rail to her masts at about 30 m. The headline hazard is depth, which leaves a short working time at the wreck.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk 10 m from the lot to the yellow stones and step down off the low rock ledge at the waterline, about one large step, onto a bottom that reaches about 4 m within a few metres of the shore. Swim 122 m west-southwest, straight out from the shore, over sand and coral heads to the dive area, and on to the buoys over the wreck. Exit at the same stones. The ledge is uneven and slippery, with sea urchins in the shallows, and turns rough when any surf runs onto it; walk the route in and out once without gear first.
* **Parking.** The hard-packed coral rubble lot beside EEG Boulevard, about 150 m north of the Trans World Radio entrance. Free, with room for many cars. Leave nothing of value in the car.
* **Access.** Open at all hours. The STINAPA Nature Fee e-tag is the only fee. Dive boats from Kralendijk visit daily and tie up to the wreck's moorings, most of them from mid-morning; the wreck is quietest in the early morning and later in the day.
* **Facilities.** None at the site.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The terrace.** Sand and scattered coral heads shelving from about 4 m at the shore to about 9 m at the dive area, 122 m out, and on at 9 to 13 m for about 70 m before the inner reef slopes away.
* **The buoys.** Three yellow buoys mark the site. Viewed from shore, the left (southeast) buoy is moored to the bow, the middle one to the reef top amidships, and the right (northwest) buoy to the stern. Descend the middle buoy's line, or follow the reef slope down from it, and the wreck comes into view on the sand below.
* **The wreck.** She lies parallel to the shore on the sand channel at the foot of the inner reef, stern to the northwest and bow to the southeast, her keel rising like a wall toward the reef and her deck facing the outer reef. Her uppermost rail, the port side, lies at about 18 m, the stern at about 21 m and the bow at about 26 m.
* **The stern.** The bronze propeller and rudder stand clear at the stern, covered in sponges. Above them, the aft deckhouse holds the galley and crew quarters, with an outside steering wheel and the funnel near the engine room.
* **Amidships and forward.** The amidships deckhouse holds the wheelhouse and chart room. Forward of each deckhouse lies a large cargo hold, the forward holds open and clear, and an anchor lies on the sand off the bow.
* **The masts.** The masts reach out across the sand channel toward the base of the outer reef, the deepest point of the dive at about 30 m.
* **The return.** Come back up the reef slope at either end of the wreck to the middle buoy, and make the safety stop on the reef top. Take a bearing to shore on the way out; the entry lies 122 m east-northeast of the dive area.

* **Drivers.** The east to southeast trade winds. The current can sit still for days, then run when the trades strengthen or shift.
* **Character.** Usually slack to light at the wreck, and at times strong enough to lengthen the swim home. Check its direction at the surface and swim to the up-current buoy first.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | southwest, 234° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | southwest, 234° |

The swim out runs 122 m west-southwest from the entry, straight out from the shore, to the dive area.

* **Bad.** South through west-northwest (S-WNW), onshore over more than 30 km of open sea. Wind from this quarter drives waves and surge straight onto the ledge and makes the entry and exit hard work.
* **Mixed.** Northwest (NW), cross-shore over more than 30 km, running a swell along the shore past the entry.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through south-southeast (NNW-SSE): offshore from north through east-southeast, off the land over 120 to 220 m, and cross-shore over 420 m from the north-northwest and over 490 m to 1.6 km from the southeast and south-southeast. The prevailing east to southeast trades blow off the land and leave the entry calm at their usual strength.

## Visibility

* **Overall.** Typically 15 to 30 m, often enough to see much of the wreck from the reef top.
* **Season.** Clearest in calm spells, most often in late summer and autumn when the trades ease.
* **Time of day.** Best in the early morning; boat traffic, divers and the afternoon breeze lower it as the day goes on.
* **Wind and swell.** Surf from south through west-northwest (S-WNW) stirs the terrace and cuts visibility on the swim out.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 26 to 29 °C year round, coolest around February and March, warmest around August through October. The water is evenly mixed down to the wreck. A dive skin, shorty or 3 mm wetsuit suits.

## Marine life

* **Tarpon.** Large tarpon live on the wreck, often several together, holding in the shade of the hull, in the holds and around the stern, and hunting in divers' lights at night.
* **The wreck.** Barracuda patrol the masts and the bow buoy line. Black groupers and graysbys shelter in the holds, with snappers, bar jacks and blue chromis in the water around the wreck and squirrelfish in the deck fittings. Sergeant majors guard egg patches on the hull, and parrotfish, angelfish and trumpetfish work the growth.
* **Growth.** Sponges in orange, yellow and purple, tube sponges, orange cup corals, star coral, sea fans and crinoids cover the upper hull and superstructure; the keel carries lighter growth.
* **Macro.** Green and spotted morays, spiny lobsters under the masts and along the hull, spider crabs, anemones with anemone shrimp, Christmas tree worms and blennies. Seahorses are seen occasionally in the growth along the rails.
* **Sand and reef.** Garden eels on the sand channel, stingrays, and occasional eagle rays over the wreck. Frogfish turn up on the reef, and octopus on the terrace.
* **Coral.** Hard coral cover along the whole leeward coast has fallen sharply since 2023 from bleaching and disease.
* **Lionfish.** Invasive lionfish are seen on the wreck.

## Hazards

* **Depth.** The upper hull lies at about 18 m and the masts at about 30 m, so bottom time at the wreck is short. Plan the turn on gas and NDL before descending, and watch depth while following the hull down.
* **Penetration.** She went down as she lay at the pier, with her fittings and cabin contents aboard. Inside are loose cables, scattered deck plates, debris in the cabins, heavy steel doors, cracked and sprung plating, deep silt and pockets of oil; keep out of the engine room. Enter only with wreck training, a light and a guide who knows the wreck, and stay in sight of an exit.
* **Shore entry.** The rock ledge at the waterline is slippery and uneven, with urchins below it. Surf from south through west-northwest (S-WNW) makes it dangerous; choose another site when any swell is running.
* **Surface swim.** The swim to the buoys is long in full gear. Snorkel out on the surface to save gas, and keep reserve for the swim home against any current.
* **Boats.** Dive boats tie up to the wreck's moorings through the day. Look up and listen before surfacing, make the safety stop on the reef top, and surface with a surface marker buoy beside the middle buoy, clear of the bow and stern moorings.
