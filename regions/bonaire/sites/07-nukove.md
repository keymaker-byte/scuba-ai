# 7. Nukove

A small, remote sandy cove on Bonaire's northwest coast, also called Doblet, reached by a long unpaved road around the BOPEC oil terminal, and the northernmost marked shore site outside Washington Slagbaai National Park. A shallow shelf of rubble and sand, left by storms where elkhorn once grew, steps down to a coral terrace and then a slope of coral ridges and sand chutes, home to nurse sharks, groupers and barracuda, on a reef that few divers make the drive to see. The entry over stones and rubble at the waterline is the headline hazard, made harder by any surf on the beach.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the small sandy beach in the cove, over stones and coral rubble at the waterline, where the footing is uneven and slippery. Storms, Hurricane Lenny in 1999 most of all, filled the narrow channel that once led through the shallows with rubble, so the way out crosses a shallow bank; look for the easiest path through it from the beach before kitting up. Fins go on and off most easily in the water, clear of the stones. Exit by the same route, which turns hard with any surf on the beach.
* **Parking.** A small unpaved lot at the end of a short track from the road, a short walk from the beach. Parking is free.
* **Access.** An hour or more from Kralendijk. Follow the coastal road north past Karpata, turn right just before the BOPEC gate onto the unpaved road around the terminal, and follow it north to the Nukove turnoff on the left, on the sea side; Past the turnoff the road continues north toward Playa Frans. The road is slow, bumpy and potholed, worst at its start beside the storage tanks, and parts wash out after heavy rain; a high clearance vehicle suits it, and a usable spare tyre is essential. The BOPEC terminal itself is closed to the public. The site lies outside Washington Slagbaai National Park, open at all hours; the Nature Fee e-tag is required. STINAPA closed every site north of Karpata on 1 May 2023 over Stony Coral Tissue Loss Disease (SCTLD), and reopened this stretch of coast, between BOPEC and Washington Slagbaai National Park, on 27 January 2024; confirm status with STINAPA before planning a dive here.
* **Facilities.** None, and no shade.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Cove.** A small sandy beach in a cove facing west, with rock formations on its north side.
* **Shallow shelf.** Rubble and sand at 4 to 5 m for about 60 m from the beach, with staghorn coral and small reef fish, a good area for the safety stop.
* **Terrace.** About 70 m out the bottom steps down to a coral terrace at 9 to 12 m, with sand patches between the coral, running out to about 180 m from the beach.
* **Slope.** Past the terrace the reef drops more steeply toward 30 m, in coral ridges separated by sand chutes, with a few small promontories and large sponges and coral heads deeper down.
* **Return.** Swim back east, 85°, across the terrace and shelf to the cove; the entry sits about 113 m from the dive area on the terrace.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | west, 266° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | west, 270° |

The swim out runs 113 m west, 265°, from the entry straight out across the shelf to the terrace.

* **Bad.** South-southwest through northwest (SSW-NW), onshore over more than 30 km of open sea. Wind from this quarter puts surf on the beach, and on the stones and rubble of the entry and exit.
* **Mixed.** South (S), cross-shore over more than 30 km, running a swell along the shore past the entry.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through south-southeast: offshore from north-northeast through southeast, and cross-shore over 320 m from the south-southeast, 490 m from the north and 1.2 km from the north-northwest. The prevailing east to southeast trades blow off the land here and leave the cove flat, and the rock formations on the north side of the cove give some shelter from the north. Swell from the north or west, from winter storms or passing tropical systems, still makes the entry harder under a light wind.

## Visibility

* **Overall.** 10 to 30 m, typically around 25 m, best on calm mornings.
* **Rain and swell.** Lower after rain and when swell stirs the shallows.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 26 to 29 °C year round, coolest around January to March, warmest around August through October. The water is evenly mixed through recreational depths. A 3 mm wetsuit suits the dive, and guards against scrapes on the entry.

## Marine life

* **Shallows.** Storms in 1999 and 2000 turned the elkhorn fields of the shallows to rubble, and the first few metres are sparse. Staghorn coral grows on the shelf, with sergeant majors, chromis and wrasse among the branches, parrotfish grazing, and octopus in holes and crevices.
* **Terrace.** Dense hard coral in good condition, with French and queen angelfish, schools of blue tang, spiny lobster, morays, porcupinefish, black crinoids and lionfish, and southern stingrays resting on the sand patches. Hawksbill turtles visit.
* **Slope.** Barrel sponges and large coral heads, groupers including Nassau grouper, large parrotfish, and barracuda along the deeper reef, with jacks hunting there in the early morning and late afternoon. Nurse sharks rest under ledges at the top of the drop-off.

## Hazards

* **Entry and exit.** Stones and coral rubble at the waterline and a shallow rubble bank beyond make the footing treacherous, and surf on the beach can throw a diver onto the rocks. Wear boots with a firm sole, and choose another site when there is surf.
* **Remote.** Mobile phone coverage is unreliable or absent, the site has no facilities, and help is a long, rough drive away. Leave a dive plan and an expected return time with someone, save the parking coordinates for emergency responders, and bring drinking water.
* **Road.** Unpaved, potholed and prone to washouts after rain, hard on tyres and low vehicles.
* **Biting insects.** Gnats at the parking area can be relentless; bring insect repellent.
* **Variable current.** Weak on most days, stronger on some; carry a surface marker buoy.
