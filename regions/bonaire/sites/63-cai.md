# 63. Cai

Cai is a shore dive on Bonaire's east coast at Lac Cai, where the deep channel through the barrier reef at the mouth of Lac Bay carries the lagoon's water out to the open sea. From a sandy beach beside the old conch shell piles, the dive follows the channel south over seagrass and sand into a sand bowl where large schools of tarpon hold, then crosses a low reef to a steep drop-off, with giant anemones in great numbers along the way. The headline hazard is the current: a seaward outflow through the channel that is often strong, changes direction and strength within a dive, and can carry a diver out to sea, with surge at the channel mouth on top of it. It is a dive for experienced divers with a local guide.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk about 50 m from the parking to the sandy beach on the seaward side of the shell piles, and wade in to knee or hip depth before putting on fins. Descend and follow the channel south (170°): the dive area lies 81 m south of the entry, and the drop-off about 280 m. The return runs north (350°) back up the channel to the same beach.
* **Parking.** Free, on the coral gravel beside the conch shell piles, with room for about 10 to 15 cars. Keep off the sand, where cars sink.
* **Access.** Lac Cai lies at the end of a bumpy, partly unpaved road around Lac Bay, signposted from the road to Sorobon; a car with good ground clearance makes it easier, and the road is worst after rain. The site is open at all hours, and the STINAPA Nature Fee e-tag is the only fee. It is a dive for advanced divers only, with an experienced local guide. Sundays bring crowds of island residents to the beach.
* **Facilities.** A snack bar on the beach, open mainly on Sundays, and a small harbour for fishing boats. There are no toilets, showers or fresh water, and little shade.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The conch shell piles.** Tall mounds of queen conch shells left from decades of harvest in Lac Bay, now protected, mark the site from the road and the beach. The shells are part of the marine park; leave them in place.
* **The shallows.** Sand and seagrass at 3 to 4 m for the first 70 m south of the entry, with seagrass flats beside the channel where turtles graze.
* **The channel.** The deep opening through the barrier reef at the bay's mouth, about 10 m wide, and the only passage for boats between Lac Bay and the sea. It deepens from about 6 m at the dive area, 81 m from the entry, to about 9 m at 120 m.
* **The sand bowl.** The channel opens into a sand bowl at 10 to 11 m, 140 to 190 m from the entry, where the tarpon school.
* **The reef ridge.** Beyond the bowl the bottom rises to a low reef at about 6 m, 200 to 240 m out.
* **The drop-off.** About 280 m south of the entry, at 11 to 12 m, the reef falls away as a steep, in places vertical wall, to about 80 m within 100 m further out. Dives run along the wall well inside recreational depth.
* **The return.** Back over the ridge and through the sand bowl, then north (350°) up the channel to the beach. Lower visibility in the channel makes a compass heading worth carrying.

## Current

| | |
|---|---|
| **Current type** | Wind driven |
| **Usual set** | south, 170°, seaward out of the channel |
| **Typical speed** | Not established |

* **Drivers.** The trade wind waves that break over the barrier reef push water into Lac Bay, and the bay drains back to sea through this channel, so a strong trade and a high swell drive a strong outflow. A falling tide adds to it. On calm days the channel can run slack.
* **Character.** The outflow is often strong in the channel and changes direction and strength within a single dive, and a side set can push a diver across the channel on the way out. Along the wall the water is at times nearly still.
* **Planning.** The outflow carries a diver out along the channel and opposes the swim back, so turn early, with a generous reserve.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | south, 184° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | south, 184° |

The swim out runs 81 m south from the entry to the dive area, 6° off the shore's facing, straight down the channel.

* **Bad.** Southeast through south (SE-S), onshore over more than 30 km of open sea. Wind from this quarter drives waves straight into the channel mouth and onto the beach, builds surge at the entry, and stands up a steep chop against the seaward outflow.
* **Short fetch.** South-southwest through west-southwest (SSW-WSW), onshore over 1.9 to 2.8 km along the coast and across the bay's mouth, enough to build a chop at the channel mouth.
* **Mixed.** East through east-southeast (E-ESE), cross-shore over more than 30 km, the quarter of the prevailing trade wind. It runs a swell past the channel mouth, breaks waves on the barrier reef and drives the outflow.
* **Fine.** West clockwise through east-northeast (W-ENE): offshore from northwest through east-northeast, off the land over 80 to 240 m, and cross-shore over 1.7 to 2.1 km from the west and west-northwest.

## Visibility

* **Overall.** 10 to 30 m, typically about 20 m, best on calm mornings.
* **The channel.** The outflow carries sediment and lagoon water out of the bay, so the channel and the water near the surface run murkier, at times only about 5 m, while the reef and the wall stay clearer. The stronger the current, the lower the visibility.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 26 to 29 °C year round, coolest around February and March, warmest around August through October. The water is evenly mixed through recreational depths. A dive skin, shorty or 3 mm wetsuit suits.

## Marine life

* **Sand bowl.** Schools of 50 to 100 tarpon hold in the sand bowl at the end of the channel, often with a school of sennet, and come close to divers who stay still.
* **Anemones.** Giant anemones in great numbers, some over a metre across, line the channel and the reef. Sun anemone shrimp, scarlet-striped cleaner shrimp and banded coral shrimp live among them.
* **Seagrass.** Green and hawksbill turtles graze the seagrass beside the channel, with queen conch and schools of bonefish over the sand.
* **Reef and wall.** Green morays, many Caribbean spiny lobsters, octopus, groupers, cubera and mutton snapper, Spanish grunts, buffalo trunkfish, Bermuda chubs and queen triggerfish. Spotted eagle rays pass over the channel and along the wall, with nurse sharks and large barracuda along the wall and jacks in the blue.

## Hazards

* **Current.** A seaward outflow through the channel, often strong, changing direction and strength within a dive, and able to carry a diver out to sea or block the swim back to the beach. Turn early, leave the reef with at least 55 to 70 bar, and end the dive at once when it runs harder than planned.
* **Surge.** Swell breaking on the barrier reef puts strong surge into the shallow channel mouth, pushing and pulling a diver in turn. Kick when it carries you forward and rest when it pulls back, keep clear of the reef, and watch the surf from the beach before kitting up.
* **Boats.** The channel is the only boat passage between Lac Bay and the sea. Carry a surface marker buoy, deploy it before surfacing, and listen for engines on the way up.
* **Remote site.** No lifeguard and few people on weekdays; help is far away. Carry a signalling device, leave the dive plan with someone ashore, and stay with your buddy and guide.
* **Depth.** The wall falls past recreational depth. Watch the computer, stay above EAN32's 33 m maximum operating depth at a ppO2 of 1.4 bar, and keep within the no-decompression limit.
* **The entry.** Broken shell and coral rubble on the beach are sharp and unstable underfoot. Wear sturdy boots.
