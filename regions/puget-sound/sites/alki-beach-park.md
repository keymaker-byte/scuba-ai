# Alki Beach Park (Junkyard)

Shore dive on the north-facing Alki shore of West Seattle, just northeast of Alki Point at the southwest entrance to Elliott Bay. A wide, sandy, gently sloping bottom off a long public beach makes this one of the easiest shore entries in the city and a standing training site, but the draw for experienced divers is the field of dumped debris out on the slope, the "junkyard," which has grown into a scattered artificial reef. The bottom carries a designated octopus preserve. The headline hazard is overhead boat traffic in shallow, popular water.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the far southwest end of the sea wall, near 64th Avenue SW, then swim directly out from shore. The beach is sandy cobblestone giving way to sand, and the entry is only metres from the road.
* **Parking.** On the street along Alki Avenue SW, on either side of the road by the southwest end of the beach. Free, but it fills through summer and on weekends, so arrive early.
* **Access.** Park hours are 05:00 to 23:00. About 18 km from central Seattle over the West Seattle Bridge, then Harbor Avenue SW and Alki Avenue SW to the southwest end of the sea wall.
* **Octopus preserve.** The site is a designated octopus preserve. Taking or harassing octopus is prohibited on the bottom here. Diving and observing are fine; a giant Pacific octopus stays where it is found.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms, benches, picnic tables, barbecue stands and a beach walkway along the park. Restaurants and shops sit across Alki Avenue. Air fills are in Seattle and Federal Way, not on site.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Swim line.** From the entry a heading of about 320° leads out across the slope and over a thick guide rope laid on the upper part of the bank.
* **Guide rope.** Following it descends across the slope to about 20 m, then curves back up the bank past piles of discarded items on the bottom, the junkyard debris that gives the site its name.
* **Old pilings.** A cluster of usually submerged piling stubs sits in the shallows about 6 m southwest of the sea wall end, then straight out from shore. They are the remains of an early-1900s boardwalk and make a good place to poke around during a safety stop.
* **Depth as a guide.** The bottom is a simple, even slope, so depth reads as distance from shore: shallow is shoreward, and the return is always up the slope and in.

## Current

Currents are usually mild here and the site dives as a benign training beach, but Alki Point sits just southwest and accelerates the flow nearby, so a slack is still worth planning. The flow floods for most of the cycle and reverses only through the second half of the ebb, which makes the slack before the flood the more reliable window; slack tends to fall about an hour before maximum ebb.

* **Flood.** Sets southwest, past the entry toward Alki Point.
* **Ebb.** Sets southwest early, then swings northwest into Elliott Bay as the ebb builds. The ebb runs stronger than the flood.
* **Diveable window.** Plan the slack before the flood and keep an eye on the point; the shallow, sandy inshore ground itself carries little current on a small exchange.

## Depth and tide

The bottom is an even slope from an eelgrass band in the shallows, across the top of the slope near 6 to 7 m, down to about 24 m below datum where it eases into a flatter grade. Elliott Bay swings 3 to 4 m between high and low water, so depths are given against the MLLW chart datum: a low reads shallower than the datum figure, a high deeper.

## Hazards

* **Boat traffic.** The main hazard. Pleasure boats and jet skis run close to shore in this popular water. Stay below about 3 m through the dive, listen for boat noise before any final ascent, and fly a dive flag, which is required here.
* **Surface chop and wakes.** Wind and the wakes of passing ships and boats both kick up the surface. On a high tide, when the water reaches the concrete sea wall, keep off the wall so an incoming wave cannot push you into it.
* **The point.** Alki Point lies just southwest and speeds up the current close to it; the flow is livelier out and to the southwest than it is on the sandy inshore ground.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces north-northwest across Elliott Bay.
* **Bad.** Northwest through northeast, with open fetch across Elliott Bay and up the main basin past the point, which builds chop on the entry.
* **Fine.** South through southeast are offshore, blowing off the West Seattle land behind the beach.
* **Wind against current.** A northwesterly opposing the southwest-setting flood, or the ebb's northwest swing into the bay, is the combination that roughens the entry most.

## Visibility

* **Recent form.** Recent reports run to a clear 6 m or more in the shallows on a high tide. Visibility here is usually workable rather than exceptional.
* **Blooms.** Spring and summer plankton blooms are the usual spoiler; a late-spring dive here has come up poor.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine sand, so it stirs easily. Careful trim and finning keep the shallows clear, and a low fin near the sand will fog the water behind you.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Around 12 °C in summer at the bottom of the slope. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** The shallow sandy flats warm above the deeper water in summer, into the low to mid teens.

## Marine life

* **Character.** A sand, eelgrass and debris site rather than a reef, so the life is spread between the eelgrass shallows, the open sand, the old pilings and the dumped junk on the slope. The man-made structure concentrates animals that otherwise have little to hold to on a plain sandy bottom.
* **Octopus.** The reason for the preserve. Giant Pacific octopus den in the debris and piling structure out on the slope, and the protected bottom is managed to keep them there.
* **Fish.** Copper, quillback and brown rockfish, lingcod, cabezon, kelp greenling and painted greenling around the debris and pilings; grunt sculpin and black-eyed gobies tucked in the junk; ratfish, sanddabs, English sole and rock sole over the sand; striped and pile perch, tube-snouts, gunnels and sand lances through the shallows.
* **Sea stars.** A wide star fauna over the sand and structure, including sunflower, morning sun, Stimpson's sun, leather, spiny, purple and equal sea stars.
* **Invertebrates.** White plumose and aggregating anemones, orange sea pens standing in the sand, light-bulb tunicates, striped and frosted nudibranchs, kelp crabs, red rock crabs, hermit crabs, moon snails and geoduck clams.
* **Eelgrass shallows.** A thick eelgrass band runs along the shallows to the top of the slope, nursery habitat that shelters small animals and holds the safety-stop interest around the old boardwalk pilings.
