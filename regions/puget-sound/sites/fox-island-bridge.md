# Fox Island Bridge

The Fox Island Bridge crosses the west end of Hale Passage, joining Fox Island to the Gig Harbor peninsula over the narrowest, shallowest water of the passage. Divers enter beside the old boat launch on Towhead Island at the Fox Island end, follow a rock jetty out, then work north from pier to pier under the bridge. The large concrete piers are covered in plumose anemones, giant barnacles and a wide range of nudibranchs, and stand over a cobble and clay bottom that deepens to the middle of the passage. The flow is pinched between the piers and the shore, so slack is short and the current returns fast; this is a dive for a small exchange, timed precisely.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the gravel beach beside the boat ramp, at the base of the rock jetty on the west side of the bridge's Fox Island end. Surface swim out along the jetty to its end and wait there for the current to ease, then swim on under the bridge and descend at the first large pier, about 230 m from the entry.
* **Parking.** A small unpaved lot at the boat launch, 2607 Fox Island Bridge Road, the first right after crossing the bridge onto the island. It has room for only a few cars in unmarked spaces, is free, and is potholed and partly eroded. Leave the ramp and the neighbouring driveways clear. The turnaround sits close to oncoming traffic on the bridge road.
* **Access.** From SR 16, take the Olympic Drive NW exit and follow the signs to Fox Island; the lot is just past the south end of the bridge. Beach access is open around the clock. Towhead Island is federal land with no managing lease, and the ramp is unmaintained. Pierce County is planning to replace the bridge; one of the alignments under study would end public access to Towhead Island, and construction would follow permitting and funding.
* **Facilities.** A concrete boat ramp, breaking apart, with small boats launching from the gravel beach beside it. There is no restroom or drinking water.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The bridge.** About 590 m long on 20 spans, running north-northeast, 15°, from Towhead Island to the mainland shore at Arletta. Smaller round piles carry the spans near each shore; the large rectangular concrete piers stand in the deeper water between them, each with a concrete ledge part way up its column.
* **The rock jetty.** Runs out from the beach on the west side of the bridge, carrying Christmas anemones and kelp crabs. It is the guide out to the bridge and the place to spend the last of the gas on the way back.
* **The inshore piles.** Between the jetty and the first large pier, on a sandy cobble bottom with drifts of shell at their bases, about 2 to 5 m below MLLW.
* **The large piers.** From the first large pier at the dive area the bottom drops pier by pier, past 10 m below MLLW some 150 m out along the bridge and to about 16 m at the middle of the passage, some 350 m out. The central piers are the largest and carry the most life, and dive like a short wall.
* **Compass work.** The next pier is often out of sight. Take a heading along the bridge, 15° true, outbound and the reciprocal, 195° true, home, and swim pier to pier on it.

## Current

The passage narrows to its shallowest at the bridge, and the exchange between Carr Inlet and the Tacoma Narrows runs fastest where the piers meet the shore.

* **Flood.** Sets west through the bridge toward Carr Inlet. Water striking the east side of the jetty turns north and runs out along it. Daily peaks run 0.43 to 0.96 m/s at the station over 30 days.
* **Ebb.** Sets east through the bridge toward the Tacoma Narrows. Water striking the west side of the jetty turns north along it, then rounds its end and turns east with the main flow. Daily peaks run 0.64 to 1.01 m/s at the station over the same 30 days.
* **Diveable window.** Both slacks are dived. At the station the window under 0.25 m/s lasts about 40 to 130 minutes at slack before flood and 50 to 160 minutes at slack before ebb, 78 minutes typical for each, longest between two weak exchanges. Under the bridge it is shorter: the flow is pinched between the piers and the shore, and the current builds quickly once it turns, carrying a diver off the bridge line into the open passage and north along the jetty, away from the beach. Plan on a small exchange, reach the end of the jetty before slack, and start back at the first sign of current.
* **The jetty.** The end of the jetty is a sheltered spot to wait for the current to ease before heading out, and the shallows along it give a lee on the return.

| **Entry shore facing** | north, 354° |

The site faces across the narrow west end of Hale Passage, about 550 m to the mainland shore, with Fox Island behind it.

* **Short fetch.** West-northwest through northwest (WNW-NW), onshore over 1.3 to 2.6 km from the mouth of Carr Inlet. Wind from these directions raises chop on the entry beach and the surface swim along the jetty.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through west (NNW-W). North-northwest through northeast is onshore but crosses under 1 km of the passage; east-northeast and east are cross-shore, along the passage, over 0.7 to 1.7 km; east-southeast through southwest is offshore, with Fox Island behind the beach; west-southwest and west are cross-shore over under 1 km.
* **Wind against current.** East through southeast (E-SE) against the ebb setting east, over 1.6 to 4.5 km; west-northwest through northwest (WNW-NW) against the flood setting west, over 1.3 to 2.6 km.

## Visibility

* **Plankton and silt.** Plankton blooms cut it through spring and summer, and the clay and silt bottom clouds easily, so it often falls short of the distance between piers. Winter generally runs clearer, and it is best near slack once the current settles.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 7 to 11 °C through the year. Drysuit.

## Marine life

* **The large piers.** Large white plumose anemones stand shoulder to shoulder on the deeper rectangular piers, with giant barnacles, short plumose anemones, sponges and limpets. Nudibranchs are the highlight, in many species: white-lined dirona, three-lined aeolid, Janolus fuscus, sea lemons, spotted dorids, clown and shaggy mouse nudibranchs.
* **The inshore piles.** Small anemones, giant acorn barnacles and tube worms cover the concrete.
* **The jetty.** Red and green Christmas anemones and many kelp crabs.
* **The bottom.** Sandy cobble with exposed clay ridges full of nooks, and drifts of shell at the base of the shallower piles. Moon snails in the shallows, sunflower and leather stars deeper, with red Irish lords, perch, flounder and penpoint gunnels.
* **Sculpins.** Buffalo sculpins are common, guarding egg masses in late winter, with scalyhead sculpins and mosshead warbonnets.

## Hazards

* **Strong current.** Slack under the bridge is short and the current returns fast, carrying a diver off the bridge line into the open passage. A late return is a long, hard swim against it.
* **The shore end of the bridge.** The current runs hardest where the bridge meets the shore, the stretch every diver crosses on the way back.
* **Navigation.** In low visibility the next pier is out of sight; without a compass heading it is easy to drift off the bridge line into the open passage.
* **Boats.** Small boats use the passage and launch from the beach beside the entry. Fewest boats and ramp users in winter.
