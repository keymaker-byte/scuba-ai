# Seacrest Cove 2

Shore dive at Seacrest Park on the West Seattle waterfront, also known as Alki Cove 2, looking straight across Elliott Bay at the downtown Seattle skyline. It is the most dived site in the city, a training beach by day and a busy night dive, with the pilings, sunken dories and steel of the old Seacrest Marina scattered down a silty slope that keeps dropping well past the recreational limit. The headline hazard is overhead traffic: the passenger ferry dock bounds the dive area on its northwest side and small boats work the shoreline all summer.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** The cobble beach immediately southeast of the Marination Ma Kai café (in the old Seacrest boathouse building) and the water taxi dock, at the 1600 block of Harbor Avenue SW. Concrete stairs flank the beach at either end, and the entry is mildly steep rock and cobble giving way to the slope. The beach faces northeast; swim out northeast toward the white boundary buoys before dropping, with the dock on your left.
* **Parking.** Curbside along Harbor Avenue SW, free. It fills early on summer weekends and around class schedules; arrive early or expect a walk.
* **Access.** From I-5, exit 163 to the West Seattle Bridge, then the Harbor Avenue exit, turn right, and follow the waterfront about 3 km to Seacrest Park. The site is also reachable without a car: the King County Water Taxi from Pier 50 downtown lands at the Seacrest dock.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms in the pier building, the Marination Ma Kai café and kayak rentals in the boathouse building, picnic tables, and a seasonal outdoor rinse shower. Boat launch ramps are at Don Armeni Park just northwest. No air fills on site.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Layout.** The dive area is the cove between the water taxi dock at its northwest end and the public fishing pier at its southeast end. Facing the water the dock and its lane are on the left and the pier on the right. Cove 3 lies a couple hundred metres northwest beyond the dock, Cove 1 southeast beyond the fishing pier toward Salty's; the coves are close enough that a day can cover more than one.
* **Boundary buoys and lines.** White buoys mark the edge of the dive area along the ferry lane, with lines run between their anchors, and a cross-cove line has been laid near 14 m. The lines are re-laid and shifted over the years, so treat any map of them as approximate and confirm the current layout on site.
* **Honey Bear.** The remains of a small cabin cruiser lie on the slope at about 8 m below MLLW, out from the entry toward the boundary line, with the dock off to their left seen from shore. The wreck is broken up and settling into the bottom, but it still shelters octopus and rockfish and remains the site's best-known landmark.
* **Marina remains.** Pilings, small sunken dories, logs and scattered debris from the old marina spread across the slope, each patch of structure heavily grown over. Bull kelp stands on some pilings in season.
* **I-beams.** Steel I-beams and girders from the marina days stand on the deeper slope, roughly 26 to 33 m down depending on tide, and are the usual deep objective.
* **Depth as a guide.** The bottom is a moderate, even, silty slope falling away to the northeast, so depth reads as distance from shore: up the slope is home in any visibility. It steepens quickly, reaching about 40 m only 150 m off the beach.

## Current

Current is not a planning factor here. The cove sits deep inside Elliott Bay, away from the main tidal streams, and stays diveable through the whole cycle on any exchange; pick the entry time for tide height, boat traffic and daylight rather than slack.

* **Strength.** Weak throughout. Station peaks stay near or under 0.2 m/s even on large exchanges, and the depth-averaged flow at the cove itself runs weaker still, so whole days pass inside a 0.25 m/s window.
* **Set.** What little stream there is runs along the shore on a roughly north-northeast to south-southwest axis, the flood side setting toward the Duwamish waterways.
* **Diveable window.** Any tide. Ship wakes and wind chop move more water at the entry than the tide does.

## Hazards

* **Water taxi.** The passenger ferry runs year-round from the dock at the northwest end of the cove, with frequent daytime sailings. A 45 m exclusion zone surrounds the dock; stay inside the buoyed dive area, and never surface in open water near the lane. Ascend up a buoy or anchor line, or stay close to the bottom all the way to shore.
* **Fishing pier.** A no-dive zone marked by yellow buoys extends around the public fishing pier at the southeast end of the cove. Keep clear of it, and of the fishing line that drapes the pier and nearby pilings. Carry a knife.
* **Boat traffic.** Small boats, jet skis and kayakers work this shoreline, heaviest in summer. Listen before any ascent and fly a dive flag; signs on site require one.
* **The slope.** The bottom keeps dropping past 40 m with nothing to arrest a drift downward, and the depth arrives quickly for a training beach. Watch the gauge, especially at night or in poor visibility.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine and stirs easily; a careless fin near the wreck or the structure fogs the water for everyone behind you.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces northeast across Elliott Bay.
* **Bad.** North through east, with fetch across the bay onto the entry, which builds chop on the beach; a northerly has the longest fetch, down the Sound and in through the bay mouth.
* **Fine.** South through southeast are offshore, blocked by the West Seattle hill behind the park. The cove is often calm when Alki Beach around the point is blown out, which makes it the standing bad-weather alternative.
* **Wakes.** With almost no tidal stream, the roughest the entry gets is a north wind stacked on the wakes of harbor traffic; time the walk out between them.

## Visibility

* **Range.** Swings from about 3 m through a bloom to 12 m or more on a good day; a recent midsummer report gave a clear 9 m at depth over 4.6 m in the shallows.
* **Blooms and silt.** Spring and summer plankton blooms are the usual spoiler, and the fine bottom stirs easily under the day's classes. The deeper slope usually reads clearer than the shallows; early in the day beats the afternoon churn.
* **Night.** A popular and rewarding night dive; the resident animals are far more active after dark, and the site's easy navigation suits it.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 8 to 13 C through the year, around 12 C at depth in summer. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the mid teens in late summer while the slope stays near 12 C; dress for the depth.

## Marine life

* **Character.** A structure site on a silty slope: nearly all the life concentrates on the marina remains, the wreck, the pilings and the I-beams, each patch a small dense reef in otherwise open silt.
* **Octopus.** Both local species. Giant Pacific octopus den in the wreck, the debris and the deeper structure, and the smaller Pacific red octopus turns up across the slope, especially after dark.
* **Fish.** Rockfish, lingcod, cabezon, kelp and painted greenlings and a broad sculpin fauna around the structure; ratfish cruise the deeper silt at night; wolf eels hold dens in the heavier structure.
* **Invertebrates.** Giant plumose anemones sheath the pilings and I-beams, with nudibranchs, sea stars, sea cucumbers, shrimp and red rock, Dungeness and kelp crabs across the structure and slope.
* **Seals.** Harbor seals haul out on the nearby floats and regularly join dives, most boldly at night.
