# Twanoh State Park

Twanoh State Park is a shore dive off the south shore of Hood Canal, on the arm running from the Great Bend toward Belfair. A gravel beach gives way to an eelgrass band and then a long, even slope of silty sand that reaches the flat floor of the canal at 27 to 32 m. The site is open bottom, a dive for animals: tube-dwelling anemones, sea pens, moon snails, hermit crabs and seasonal swarms of jellies, and fish are sparse. The current is negligible on every exchange; the headline hazards are boats from the park's launch and mooring buoys, and low oxygen in the deep water in late summer and autumn.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk from the lot onto the beach and wade in over cobble, then gravel, then silty sand. Surface swim out past the roped swimming area and descend in the eelgrass beyond it. The east end of the park, toward the pier, has the steeper slope and the better diving. On a low tide the walk to swimming depth crosses a wide stretch of cobble.
* **Parking.** The day-use lots sit between Highway 106 and the beach. A Discover Pass is required: $10 a day or $45 a year.
* **Access.** The park is open 6:30 a.m. to dusk in summer and 8 a.m. to dusk in winter, at 12190 E State Route 106, about 13 km southwest of Belfair. Pack out all waste from the day-use area. The shoreline and the mouth of Twanoh Creek were restored in summer 2026. Shellfish harvesting needs a state licence and closes at times for biotoxins; oysters are shucked on the beach.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms with changing areas, showers near the campground, picnic tables and covered kitchen shelters built of log and stone by the Civilian Conservation Corps in the 1930s, a pier with a floating dock, a roped swimming area, a boat ramp with launch docks at the west end, seven mooring buoys, and a restaurant by the beach open in summer.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The beach.** Cobble at the top of the beach turns to gravel and then silty sand in the shallows. The swimming area is roped off the beach with lines and floats; swim around it on the surface.
* **Eelgrass.** A band of eelgrass runs the length of the park at about 1 to 4 m below MLLW, with oyster beds inshore of it.
* **The pier.** The pier runs 92 m out from shore on a bearing of 31°, its base 156 m southeast of the entry. The site coordinate lies 187 m due north of its end.
* **The slope.** On the 45° bearing from the entry, the bottom reaches 1.8 m below MLLW 56 m out, 4.2 m at 111 m, 7.9 m at 167 m and 12.9 m at the site coordinate, 222 m out. It continues to 18.2 m at 278 m and 23.2 m at 333 m, then levels onto the floor of the canal at about 27 to 28 m some 440 m from the entry, dipping to about 32 m in places to the north. Below about 18 m the bottom is fine silt, with sparser life.
* **Tube anemones.** The tube-dwelling anemones gather from about 9 to 12 m below MLLW. Swim down the slope to about 12 m, then follow it west, angling back in to about 9 m while scanning the bottom.
* **Sand dollars.** A field of sand dollars lies on the slope northeast of the boat ramp.
* **Boat ramp.** The single boat ramp sits at the west end of the park, 307 m west-southwest of the entry. Stay well clear of it.
* **Getting home.** The slope runs along the shore, so the way home is always upslope. Come back up to the eelgrass, then follow 225°, the reciprocal of the 45° swim out, to the beach, and ascend in the shallows or inside the roped swimming area, away from boat traffic.

## Current

Twanoh is dived independently of the tidal current. The water here moves at 0.10 m/s or less on every exchange, far under any speed that would close the site.

* **Flood.** Sets east-northeast along the shore toward Lynch Cove, peaking at 0.06 to 0.10 m/s.
* **Ebb.** Sets west-southwest along the shore toward the Great Bend, peaking at 0.04 to 0.09 m/s.
* **Diveable window.** Any time of day, on either exchange. Peak current stays far under 0.25 m/s on every exchange, so nothing narrows it.
* **Slack.** This reach of the canal behaves as a standing wave, so slack falls near high and low water, about 40 minutes before each high and low at Lynch Cove Dock.
* **Drift.** A light drift along the slope carries the jellies in the water column past a diver.

The beach is a straight shore facing across the arm of the canal toward the Kitsap Peninsula, 1.8 to 2.4 km away. The arm runs east-northeast to west-southwest, so the longest reach of water opens up toward Lynch Cove.

* **Bad.** East-northeast (ENE), onshore over 6.0 km down the arm from Lynch Cove, and builds chop straight onto the beach and the surface swim.
* **Short fetch.** North-northwest through northeast (NNW-NE), onshore over 1.8 to 4.8 km across the arm, and east (E), onshore over 1.1 km, both straight onto the beach and the surface swim.
* **Fine.** East-southeast clockwise through northwest (ESE-NW): offshore from south-southeast through west, off the land behind the park, and cross-shore over 300 m to 1.9 km from east-southeast, southeast, west-northwest and northwest.
* **Wind against current.** The current is too weak to stack against a wind. Wind alone decides the surface, so read the forecast for the beach.

## Depth and tide

The south end of Hood Canal carries one of the largest tides in the region, and it decides both how far the walk out runs and how deep every feature reads. A big high adds more than 4 m to every depth; a minus tide uncovers the top of the eelgrass and brings the tube anemone band up to 7.6 to 10.6 m.

## Visibility

* **Layers.** The water is strongly layered. A fresh surface layer often holds hazy, chunky water with algae in the top few metres; visibility usually opens to 3 to 6 m on the slope below it, then turns muddy toward the floor of the canal, below about 26 m.
* **Season.** Typically 1.5 to 6 m. The clearest days, 6 m and more, come in late winter and early spring. Plankton blooms in spring and summer and winter rain on the surface layer both cut it.
* **Jellies.** Through winter and spring small jellies fill the water column from the surface to about 15 m, thick enough at times to obstruct visibility.
* **Silt.** The slope is fine silt, and a careless fin kick hangs in the still water for a long time.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 8 to 11 °C through the year. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** Sharply different from depth. In summer the shallows warm to among the warmest saltwater in the state, about 15 to 18 °C. In winter a fresh surface layer can chill to near freezing above a sharp thermocline a few metres down, with about 10 °C below it. Numb hands and face on the surface swim are normal in winter; descend early and stay under the cold layer.

## Marine life

* **Eelgrass.** Blackeye gobies, small perch, tube-snouts, bay pipefish and threespine sticklebacks, with oysters, mussels and small crabs in the shallows.
* **Sand and silt.** Tube-dwelling anemones in numbers below about 9 m, sea pens, giant plumose anemones, Lewis moon snails and their egg collars, sand dollars, cockles, geoducks, sea cucumbers and red sea squirts.
* **Sea stars.** Sunflower, mottled, leather, giant pink, ochre and sand stars, and Stimpson's sun stars.
* **Crabs.** Hermit crabs of many kinds in great numbers, including Bering and blackeye hermits, with graceful, red rock and Dungeness crabs in the shallows down to about 9 m.
* **Nudibranchs.** Giant nudibranchs (Dendronotus iris) and pink tritonias on the deeper slope from about 20 m, opalescent and other aeolid nudibranchs, and brown dorids.
* **Fish.** Sparse. Starry flounder and other flatfish on the sand, and ratfish on the deeper slope.
* **Octopus.** Small red octopus hiding in empty shells.
* **Jellies.** The site's highlight from winter through spring: fried egg, cross, water, aggregating, thimble and tailed jellies, sea gooseberries and lobed comb jellies.

## Hazards

* **Boat traffic.** Boats launch from the ramp at the west end and use the park's seven mooring buoys, and small boats and water skiers run along the shore. Fly a dive flag, keep a gas reserve to stay down until the shallows, listen for engines before ascending, and come up inside the swimming area, beside a piling or in the shallows. Stay clear of the boat ramp.
* **Depth creep.** The slope is long and featureless and runs to the floor of the canal at 27 to 32 m below MLLW, deeper than 36 m on a big high. Watch the computer and turn on the no-decompression limit. On EAN32, stay above 33 m.
* **Low oxygen.** Southern Hood Canal loses its oxygen at depth in late summer and autumn, in some years to near zero at the bottom off the park. Animals leave the deep water, the bottom can carry white bacterial mats and dead animals, and fish kills wash up on the beaches. Leave stressed animals alone.
* **Cold surface layer.** In winter the fresh surface water can sit near freezing above much warmer water at depth. Exposed skin goes numb within minutes; keep the surface swim short and wear gloves and a hood.
* **Silt and layers.** Silt, algae and jellies can drop visibility to near nothing in the top layer and near the bottom. Stay close to a buddy and carry a torch.
* **Bacteria.** In hot summer spells the beach has been posted with swimming advisories for high fecal bacteria counts. Check Washington Ecology's BEACH program before a summer dive.
* **Minus tides.** A big low leaves a long walk out and back over cobble and soft bottom in full gear.
