# Point White Dock (Crystal Springs Pier)

Shore dive off a historic Mosquito Fleet steamer dock on the southwest shore of Bainbridge Island, just north of Point White where Rich Passage opens into Port Orchard. A gentle sand bottom runs out under the dock to only a few metres at its end, then drops steeply into Port Orchard, and the pilings, an old cartwheel past the dock end and the sand around them carry most of the life in the top 9 m. Sand dollar beds, sea pens and a busy mix of crabs, stars and nudibranchs set it apart from most sandy beach sites, and the reason is the current that sweeps the point. That current is the headline hazard: it runs along the shore and turns on a schedule that does not track predictions closely here.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Climb over two or three drift logs to the sand beside the dock and walk in. Facing out along the dock, enter on the left (south) side on a flood and on the right (north) side on an ebb, so you start up-current. At high tide the walk is a few steps and there is no surface swim; at low tide the walk out is longer across the sand dollar bed, so step around the dark purple live ones (the gray ones are dead).
* **Parking.** Free roadside parking at the pier on Crystal Springs Drive NE, room for only a handful of cars.
* **Access.** Bainbridge Island Metro Park and Recreation District land, a 0.1 ha park whose listed amenities are fishing and scuba diving. Open dawn to dusk. 3949 Crystal Springs Drive NE, Bainbridge Island, about 9 km from the Winslow ferry terminal by way of Lynwood Center and Point White Drive.
* **Facilities.** The pier itself; no restrooms.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The dock.** The pilings run straight out from the entry about 85 m to the dock end, a built-in guide out and back. Barnacles, mussels and plumose anemones cover them, and fish shelter among them.
* **The cartwheel.** An old cartwheel lies on the bottom just past the end of the dock, decorated with orange and white plumose anemones.
* **The drop.** Beyond the dock end the sand steepens sharply, losing roughly a metre of depth for every five to six metres out. Most of the life is above about 9 m; the deeper slope is open and plain.
* **Bottles.** The Mosquito Fleet called here for decades, so old glass turns up in the sand around the dock.

| **Time offset** | Subtract 30 minutes from the station's slack, then confirm by looking at the water before entering |
| **Flood axis** | southwest, 238° |
| **Ebb axis** | northeast, 55° |

* **Flood.** At the dock the flood sets north, about 345°, along the shore up Port Orchard, while the station out in Rich Passage reports it heading southwest into Sinclair Inlet. Peak speeds at the station run 1.2 to 1.8 m/s; along the dock the current is far weaker but still enough to make holding a position over the shallow sand hard work.
* **Ebb.** Sets south, about 165°, along the shore back toward Rich Passage.
* **Diveable window.** Either slack. The point sits where Rich Passage, Port Orchard and Sinclair Inlet meet, and slack here runs anywhere from about 20 to 40 minutes ahead of the station, so treat the offset as a starting point. Arrive early, watch the water along the pilings, and be ready to wait or call the dive. With a slight current running, start west of the pilings and work up-current first.

## Depth and tide

The dock sits in very shallow water, so the tide changes the dive under it more than the depth figure suggests: at a big low the dock end is barely 2 m deep, at a high it is over 7 m.

## Hazards

* **Current.** Real, and timing it off a prediction alone is unreliable here. Look at the water before kitting up and abort if the pilings show a set.
* **Shallow depth.** Much of the dive is in 2 to 9 m. Weight to stay down in the shallows, where the life is, and watch buoyancy on the ascent from so little depth.
* **Depth creep.** The slope past the dock end drops steeply and keeps going past 30 m; it is easy to follow it deeper than planned.
* **Boat and fishing traffic.** The pier is a fishing and crabbing spot, so expect lines and crab pots around the pilings, and small boats rounding Point White. Carry a cutting tool and fly a marker buoy.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces west-southwest across Port Orchard toward Bremerton, with Port Orchard running north and Rich Passage opening to the south and east.
* **Bad.** North and south winds blow along Port Orchard's length and build chop over the longest fetch; west and southwest winds blow straight onshore across a few kilometres of open water.
* **Fine.** East and northeast, offshore and blocked by the island behind the beach.
* **Wind against current.** A south wind opposing the flood's northward set along the shore, or a north wind against the ebb's southward set, is the roughest combination.

## Visibility

* **Seasonal.** Follows the Sound's pattern: worst through spring and summer plankton blooms, better from late autumn through winter.
* **Bottom.** The fine sand stirs easily in the shallows, so good buoyancy and keeping off the bottom preserve visibility for the dive back.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 7 to 11°C through the year, in line with the central Sound. Standard drysuit protocol year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the low teens late in summer, and the shallow water under the dock runs warmest of all.

## Marine life

* **Sand.** Sand dollar beds, juvenile sea pens with brown-striped nudibranchs feeding on them, tube-dwelling anemones, moon snails, sea cucumbers, razor clams and geoducks, flounder, hermit crabs, Dungeness crabs and many red rock crabs. Skates are occasionally reported.
* **Pilings and cartwheel.** Orange and white plumose anemones, giant barnacles, mussels, small pink and purple tube worms, and sunflower, leather, mottled and purple stars heaped on the pilings. Leafy hornmouth snails and coonstripe shrimp work the base.
* **Fish.** Blue striped seaperch, tube-snouts and gunnels among the pilings.
* **Nudibranchs.** Brown-striped nudibranchs on the sea pens, and the pink scoop-shovel nudibranch.
* **Algae and drifters.** Sea lettuce and iridescent blue ribbon kelp, and moon jellies in the water column.
