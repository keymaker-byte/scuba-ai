# Fox Island West Wall

Shore dive at the end of Kamus Drive on the southwest shore of Fox Island, at the mouth of Carr Inlet in the South Puget Sound. A cobble beach gives way to beds of purple sand dollars, then a steep sand slope drops toward a low ledge of clay and rock about 14 to 16.5 m below MLLW, short and easy to miss, its holes and crevices home to octopus, warbonnets, sculpins and a large cast of nudibranchs. The beach lies out of the main stream between the Tacoma Narrows and Carr Inlet, and the current here is usually mild. Its headline hazard is the slope itself: a diver who follows it down past the ledge keeps going past 30 m.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk from the car past the large flat boulders across the road end, which make a staging area, and down onto the cobble beach. The surface swim is 30 m or less. Descend over the cobble, which gives way to sand near the low-water line.
* **Parking.** Free, on the shoulder of Kamus Drive above the road end, with room for about four to six cars. The turnaround at the bottom of the hill is posted no parking; leave it clear for cars to turn, and keep out of the driveways on either side. Carpool where possible.
* **Access.** From Highway 16 west of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge, take the Olympic Drive NW exit and follow the signs to Fox Island. Cross the Fox Island Bridge and follow the main road to 9th Avenue, turn right, then right again onto Kamus Drive, and follow it down a very steep hill to the water. The road end is a public right of way to the shore, open at any hour, between private homes; keep noise down, especially gearing up and down for a night dive, stay within the right of way, and pack out all garbage.
* **Facilities.** None. This is a residential road end.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The shallows.** Cobble at the waterline changes to sand near the low-water line, and the sand runs out to the top of the slope at about 1 m below MLLW. Beds of purple sand dollars cover the sand here, dense enough to look like black velvet from above, with patches of eelgrass among them.
* **The slope.** Past the sand dollar beds the bottom drops off abruptly and runs down as a steep sand slope. It falls off to the west, so a diver holding a compass heading toward the ledge has the bottom sloping away to the right the whole way, and feels off course.
* **The ledge.** A crescent of low ledge, clay and rock with boulders along it, about 2 to 3 m high and short enough to swim end to end several times in one dive. Its top lies at about 14 m below MLLW and its base at about 16.5 m. The southeast part runs parallel to shore; the northwest end, near the dive site coordinate, curves back east-northeast toward the entry. Sand builds up against it and buries part of its face, and it stands tallest in winter. Rocks scattered on the sand slope mean the ledge is close.
* **Finding the ledge by compass.** From the entry, swim out on a compass heading of 200 to 215° and hold it down the slope; 200° leads to the middle of the ledge, and 180° to its southeast end. Following the slope down instead of the heading leads past the ledge into deep water.
* **Finding the ledge by depth.** Swim straight out from the entry and down to about 12 m below MLLW, then turn left (southeast) and follow that contour; the ledge lies a few minutes along it. A diver who misses it on the heading can do the same from wherever they reach that depth.
* **Buoys.** Mooring buoys off the beach help line up the swim; the ledge lies to the left of them as seen from the entry.
* **The sea pen fields.** South and east of the ledge, and down the slope beyond it, the sand holds fields of orange sea pens. Their extent changes from year to year.
* **The boiler.** A large steel boiler from an old wreck, about 13 m long and 3.4 m across, lies on the slope at about 16 to 20 m below MLLW, about 100 m south-southeast along the shore from the road end, in front of a short seawall of pilings driven into the beach below abandoned beach cabins. It lies east to west, with cutouts in its sides and octopus dens beneath it. It is charted as a wreck. It is a separate dive from the ledge, entered from the beach in front of the seawall, about a 50 m swim out; reaching it from the ledge along the contour is a long swim.
* **The way back.** Return up the slope on the reciprocal heading, toward the northeast, and come up through the sand dollar beds to the road end. Surface close to shore and check position against the boulders at the road end before swimming in.

The beach sits out of the main stream between the Tacoma Narrows and Carr Inlet, and on most tides the water over the slope and the ledge barely moves.

* **Flood.** Sets northwest along the shore into Carr Inlet, weak at the site. At the station it is the stronger of the two, with daily peaks of 0.97 to 1.84 m/s over 30 days.
* **Ebb.** Sets southeast along the shore toward Gibson Point, also weak at the site. At the station, daily peaks run 0.59 to 1.34 m/s over the same 30 days.
* **Diveable window.** Most tides are diveable away from slack, and the site stays diveable well into a running tide. For the stillest water, dive near either slack; a typical window under 0.25 m/s at the station runs about 50 to 70 minutes, narrower on a big exchange.
* **Unexpected current.** On some days a current runs along the ledge strong enough to need a hand-over-hand pull along the bottom, independent of the predicted current, and strongest at the southeast end of the ledge. A mild set along the slope can also persist long after slack. Watch the sand and particles in the water on the way down, and turn the dive back toward the entry if the water starts to move.

## Wind

|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | west-southwest, 240° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | west-southwest, 242° |

The beach faces west-southwest across the mouth of Carr Inlet toward McNeil Island, with the open South Sound to the south and Carr Inlet running away to the northwest.

* **Bad.** South (S), onshore over 8.1 km of open water from the South Sound, and west through west-northwest (W-WNW), onshore over 6.8 to 9.4 km across Carr Inlet. Both push chop and small breakers onto the cobble beach, which stirs up the shallows and makes the entry and exit rough.
* **Short fetch.** South-southwest through west-southwest (SSW-WSW), onshore over 3.0 to 3.8 km across to McNeil Island, enough to build a chop on the entry.
* **Mixed.** South-southeast (SSE), cross-shore over 7.7 km, and northwest (NW), cross-shore over 11.4 km down Carr Inlet, each running a swell along the beach past the entry.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through southeast (NNW-SE), offshore off the island behind the beach, or cross-shore over 1.1 km or less.
* **Wind against current.** West-northwest through northwest (WNW-NW), over 9.4 to 11.4 km, blows against the northwest-setting flood, and southeast through south (SE-S), over 1.1 to 8.1 km, against the southeast-setting ebb. The water at the site usually runs too slowly to stack a chop of its own; it matters on the days the current picks up.

## Depth and tide

South Sound tides are large, and the tide swings the depth here by up to about 6 m. On a big minus tide the sand dollar beds dry out to the top of the slope, and the exposed bottom in front of the beach is soft mud.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Usually 3 to 6 m on the ledge, and often worse in the shallows. Best from autumn through early spring, worst in the spring and summer plankton blooms, and cut by heavy rain and runoff. Wind chop and breaking waves on the beach cloud the shallows, and the fine sand and silt on the slope stir up easily. In summer the water below the thermocline, a few metres down, can be clearer than at the surface.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 9 °C in late winter to about 12 to 13 °C in late summer and early autumn. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms well above the water at depth in summer, with a sharp thermocline a few metres down.

## Marine life

* **On the ledge.** Giant Pacific octopus and red octopus in dens along the face, marked by piles of crab shells at their entrances, with occasional wolf eels. Copper and brown rockfish gather in numbers and come close to divers. Lingcod guard egg masses in winter, smoothhead sculpins guard eggs of their own, and great sculpins grow very large here, alongside buffalo, grunt and sailfin sculpins, painted greenlings, mosshead and decorated warbonnets, orange gunnels and slender cockscombs. Giant pink sea stars sit on top of the ledge, along with the shell-and-spine tubes of basket-top spaghetti worms, and jelly polyps hang under the overhangs. Sponges, orange plumose anemones and very large California sea cucumbers grow on the rock.
* **Nudibranchs.** The site's richest group. White-lined dironas in great numbers in summer, Monterey and noble sea lemons, diamondback tritonias (mating and laying eggs in large numbers in some winters), pink tritonias, red dendronotids, giant nudibranchs, Nanaimo and leopard dorids, white berthellas, Cockerell's dorids, three-lined aeolids, opalescent nudibranchs, Ancula and hooded nudibranchs.
* **In the shallows.** Beds of purple sand dollars in a range of sizes, hermit crabs, moon snails, flounder and Pacific sanddabs, with bay pipefish and spiny lumpsuckers in the eelgrass.
* **On the sand slope.** Orange sea pens, often grazed by striped nudibranchs, tube-dwelling and swimming anemones, small white sea cucumbers, clam siphons, sturgeon poachers, northern spearnose poachers and plainfin midshipman. Sea stars throughout include pink, sunflower (juveniles included), leather, mottled, rose, slime, blood, vermilion and Stimpson's sun stars.
* **In the water column.** Schools of tubesnouts, striped and pile perch over the ledge, dogfish passing along the slope, and large jellies in late summer.

## Hazards

* **Depth below the ledge.** The slope keeps dropping past 30 m below MLLW, and at high water the base of the ledge reads over 21 m on the gauge. A diver who misses the ledge and follows the slope down runs deep quickly. Set a turn depth and watch the computer.
* **Missing the ledge.** The ledge is short and easy to swim past in poor visibility. Hold the compass heading, or use the depth contour, and turn back toward the entry rather than searching deeper.
* **Unexpected current.** Usually mild, but on some days strong along the ledge, strongest at its southeast end, independent of the predicted current. Turn the dive when the water starts to move.
* **Boat traffic.** Small boats run and moor off the beach. Listen for engines, ascend along the slope, and surface close to shore.
* **Minus tides.** On a big minus tide the flat in front of the beach is soft mud that is hard to cross in gear.
* **Silt.** The sand and silt on the slope stir up easily. Stay off the bottom along the ledge.
* **The hill.** Kamus Drive climbs very steeply from the beach. Take the walk up slowly after the dive, since heavy exertion soon after a dive raises the risk of decompression sickness.
