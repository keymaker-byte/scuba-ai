# Tolmie State Park (Tolmie Barges)

Shore dive at Tolmie State Park on the south shore of Nisqually Reach, between Johnson Point and the Nisqually River delta northeast of Olympia. A wide, nearly level sand flat runs out from the beach to a slope that drops onto the sand of an underwater park of about 11 ha, where three wooden barges sunk in 1965 as an artificial reef lie about 600 m offshore, marked by a buoy. The barges are thick with anemones, crabs and perch, and the sand around them holds sea pens and a large bed of sand dollars. The headline hazard is the distance: a surface swim of about 600 m each way in open water with boat traffic, to partly collapsed barges that are easily lost in poor visibility.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Gear up at the car and walk about 100 m to the beach, crossing the footbridge over the creek estuary onto the gravel and sand spit. Enter in front of the footbridge. At high tide the water is deep enough to swim almost from the waterline, and the surface swim out to the barges takes about 20 to 30 minutes. At low tide the flat dries far out and the swim shortens to the slope, after a long walk over sand; at a big minus tide the walk reaches nearly 400 m. A tide in between balances the two. Walk and fin carefully over the flat, which is covered in sand dollars.
* **Parking.** The lower lot at the bottom of the hill, a short walk from the beach. A Discover Pass is required, available as a one-day or annual pass, with an automated pay station in the park.
* **Access.** From I-5, take Exit 111 (Marvin Road, State Route 510) and drive north on Marvin Road NE about 6 km, through two roundabouts, to where the main road curves sharply left. Continue straight to where Marvin Road NE ends at 56th Avenue NE, turn right, and follow it to the park. Just before the entrance the road forks; keep left and follow the road down the hill to the lower lot. Washington State Parks runs the park as day use only, open 8 a.m. to dusk in summer and 9 a.m. to dusk in winter, and closed on Mondays and Tuesdays through the winter season. The public beach ends at the park boundary, and the shore on either side is private. Boats can tie up to the park's mooring buoys for a fee, and the barges are often dived from a boat.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms, drinking water, a faucet in the parking lot for rinsing gear, picnic tables and shelters, two kitchen shelters, fire pits and grills, and hiking trails through the wooded park.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The footbridge.** A 29 m span bridge over the creek that feeds the tidal lagoon behind the beach. It is the entry and the starting point for the compass heading.
* **The flat.** Nearly level sand, sea lettuce and sand dollars from the beach out about 400 m, sloping gently to about 1.3 m below MLLW at its outer edge. It is awash about 300 m out at a zero tide and dries farther out on a minus tide. A good snorkel at high tide, and a long, featureless swim on scuba.
* **The slope.** Between about 400 and 550 m out on the line to the barges, the bottom drops from about 1 m to about 14 m below MLLW, then flattens onto the sand around the barges.
* **The heading.** Swim out from the footbridge on a compass heading of about 035° (050° true) toward the marker buoy. The buoy is white with faded orange bands and small at this distance; at high tide it is hard to pick out from the water until halfway out. Take the heading on shore before entering.
* **The shallow barge.** The innermost of the three, inshore of the other two on the line out from the footbridge, in shallower water. Its position and depth are not established.
* **The intermediate barge.** At about 47.12453, -122.77040, about 590 m from the entry on a bearing of 047°, west-northwest of the buoy. It stands about 2.5 m off a seabed about 14 m below MLLW, its top about 11.5 m.
* **The outer barge.** At about 47.12417, -122.76964, about 610 m from the entry on a bearing of 054° and 70 m southeast of the intermediate barge. It lies beside the buoy's mooring block, its top about 12 m below MLLW, and the seabed along its outside (east) wall about 16 m, the deepest part of the dive.
* **North of the buoy.** A larger, more intact wooden barge lies a long swim north of the buoy, past scattered tires, with holes rotted through its hull into the compartments. Its position is not established.
* **Compass deviation.** Metal in and around the barges pulls a compass off near them. Take bearings a few metres clear of the wreckage.
* **The way back.** Reverse the heading, about 215° on the compass (230° true), up the slope and across the flat to the footbridge. Ascend up or beside the buoy line and check for boats before starting the swim in.

## Current

The barges lie in a shallow bay off the main stream of Nisqually Reach, and the water over them moves slowly.

* **Flood.** Sets west-northwest across the bay, toward the private shore beyond the park's western boundary. Daily peaks run 0.53 to 1.07 m/s at the station over 30 days; over the barges it stays far weaker, about 0.1 m/s or less near the bottom.
* **Ebb.** Sets east-southeast, toward the private shore east of the park. Daily peaks run 0.41 to 1.11 m/s at the station over the same 30 days, again far weaker over the barges.
* **Diveable window.** Either slack. Current at the station stays under 0.25 m/s for 54 to 144 minutes at slack before flood, typically about 90 minutes, and 84 to 138 minutes at slack before ebb, typically about 100 minutes. On the weakest exchanges of the month it stays under that for up to about 6 hours. Big exchanges around new and full moon give the shortest windows. The current over the barges is light on most tides, and timing the dive near slack matters most for the surface swim.
* **At the surface.** The current runs faster at the surface out by the buoy than near the beach, and a diver on the surface on a running tide drifts along the shore, east-southeast on the ebb and west-northwest on the flood, lengthening the swim back to the park beach.
* **Near slack.** The current around the barges swings direction as it turns; keep a compass heading near slack.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | northeast, 050° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | north, 005° |

The beach faces northeast across Nisqually Reach toward Anderson Island and the Key Peninsula. The swim out runs straight off the beach along its facing.

* **Bad.** North-northeast (NNE), onshore over 9.4 km of open water, and east through east-southeast (E-ESE), onshore over 7.2 to 8.3 km along Nisqually Reach. Either one builds chop on the beach and on the long surface swim.
* **Short fetch.** North (N), onshore over 4.8 km, and northeast through east-northeast (NE-ENE), onshore over 3.7 to 3.9 km. Each raises a chop on the swim and over the shallow flat.
* **Mixed.** North-northwest (NNW), cross-shore over 19.1 km of open water, running a swell along the beach and across the swim.
* **Fine.** Southeast clockwise through northwest, offshore off the wooded shore and bluffs behind the park, or cross-shore over 2.0 km or less. The beach lies in the lee of southerly storm winds.
* **Wind against current.** West-northwest through north-northwest (WNW-NNW) blows against the flood, and east through east-southeast (E-ESE) against the ebb. The current over the barges is too weak to raise chop of its own; the concern is the surface swim, and the main stream farther out in the reach.

## Depth and tide

South Sound tides are large, and the tide swings the depth here by up to about 6 m. At a big minus tide the flat dries nearly 400 m out from the beach, close to the top of the slope.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Usually about 1 to 3 m, at best about 6 m. The fine silt on the bottom clouds at the touch of a fin, so the visibility drops quickly around a group of divers; stay a little off the bottom. Worst in the spring and summer plankton blooms. The Nisqually River delta lies a few kilometres east, and the river's silt load runs highest in winter. In poor visibility the barges are easy to miss from just a few metres away.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 7 to 11 °C through the year. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** The shallow flat warms well above the water at depth on sunny days from spring through summer.

## Marine life

* **On the barges.** White and orange plumose anemones and swimming anemones cover the timbers, with a variety of nudibranchs. Red rock and Dungeness crabs are common on the barges, striped perch school around the hulls, and rockfish, sculpins and the occasional lingcod shelter in the wreckage. Octopus and wolf eels den in the barges and the debris around them.
* **On the sand around the barges.** Sea pens, sea stars, moon snails, hermit crabs, geoduck clams and the occasional skate.
* **On the flat.** A large bed of sand dollars, sea lettuce, moon snails and hermit crabs, and crabs foraging over the sand.

## Hazards

* **The surface swim.** About 600 m each way across open water, 20 to 30 minutes at high tide, and longer against a running tide or a headwind. Plan fitness and a gas reserve for the swim back, and keep reserve buoyancy for resting on the surface.
* **Boat traffic.** Small boats run across the bay and tie up to the park's mooring buoys near the barges. Fly a dive flag, listen for engines, and ascend up or beside the buoy line with a hand overhead.
* **Collapsed barges.** The barges have partly collapsed, and their timbers, holes and loose debris snag gear and divers. Stay outside the wreckage.
* **Drift along the shore.** On a running tide the surface current carries a diver along the shore toward private beaches on either side of the park. Dive near slack and keep the footbridge on the heading.
* **Compass deviation.** Metal around the barges throws a compass off. Take the heading back to shore clear of the wreckage.
* **Tire bundles and fishing line.** About 3,900 tires were removed from the old tire reef beside the park in 2024. Stray tires and banding can remain on the bottom, with monofilament fishing line snagged on them.
* **Silt.** The bottom stirs up quickly and cuts the visibility to almost nothing around the barges. Good buoyancy and a reel or a fixed compass heading keep a team together.
