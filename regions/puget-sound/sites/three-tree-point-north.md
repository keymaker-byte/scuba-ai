# Three Tree Point (North)

Shore dive on the north side of Three Tree Point, a wooded residential point that juts into East Passage on the Burien shore of Puget Sound, south of Seattle. The draw is an artificial reef: tire piles, mounds of concrete and PVC pipe, a satellite dish, household junk and several small sunken boats scattered across a sand and cobble slope, all of it thick with fish. The reef itself sits shallow, but the slope falls away fast and continues past 40 m, so depth control is the standing concern. Current is usually mild, wrapping clockwise around the point and setting toward it, and the shallows carry more of it than the depths. It dives well by day and comes alive at night.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the public beach access at the end of SW 170th Street, a gated dirt path that runs down between private houses to a concrete platform above the beach. Enter directly in front of where the path meets the water. The beaches on either side are private, so stay off them.
* **Parking.** A small, unmarked paved area by a private residence at the head of the path, with room for only four or five cars. It fills during salmon fishing season. Watch for No Parking signs on the approach, which belong to the neighbours and to Three Tree Point South around the curve.
* **Access.** Dawn to dusk, posted with a fine for violators. About 18 km south of central Seattle by way of Highway 518 west through Burien, then Ambaum Boulevard, SW 152nd Street and the long, winding descent of Maplewild Avenue SW to SW 170th Street.
* **Facilities.** None on site; this is a quiet residential neighbourhood, so keep noise down, especially at night, and do not block driveways. Air fills are in Kent, Federal Way and Tacoma.

## Navigation and landmarks

The reef is spread across a broad area and is easy to miss piecemeal, so most dives pick one direction and work it. The buoys offshore of the entry mark the descent; drop there and follow the slope.

* **To the point (southwest).** Descend the slope and follow the bottom contour southwest toward the point, on a heading of about 210°. Two clusters of tires lie along the 13 m and 18 m contours, encrusted with tube worms and sea squirts, and a small cabin cruiser of about 7 m sits with its bow near 14 m, its hull cased in barnacles and small white anemones. A Fiberform pleasure boat lies deeper, near 21 m on the southwest side.
* **To the junkyard (northeast).** Turning right along the shore instead leads to the densest debris: mounds of corrugated concrete and PVC pipe full of fish, old appliances and lawn furniture, a satellite dish, a broken canoe and a runabout still on its trailer.
* **Substrate.** Sandy cobble in the shallows, turning to silty sand at the top of the slope where eelgrass grows between about 5 and 8 m, then back to sandy cobble partway down. Below roughly 27 m it is silt and sand with little structure.
* **Return and safety stop.** Come back up the slope and drift the shallows toward the entry, finishing with a safety stop over the sand, where the small animals reward a slow look.

| **Flood axis** | south, 186° |
| **Ebb axis** | north-northwest, 343° |

* **Flood.** In the channel the flood sets south down East Passage; along the north side of the point it wraps clockwise and runs southwest toward the point, parallel to shore. The flood is the stronger of the two exchanges here.
* **Ebb.** The channel ebb sets north-northwest, and back-eddies form off the point. Because the ebb is the gentler exchange, the slack before the ebb and the ebb itself are the calmer times to dive.
* **Where the current sits.** It is usually mild and often unnoticeable at depth, and stronger in the shallows. Staying toward the northeast, junkyard end keeps you out of most of it, and the water builds as you work southwest toward the point. The junkyard is sheltered on an ebb, while the straight-out ground toward the point is best on slack or a mild ebb. Strong current is rare and comes with large tidal exchanges; on a big exchange, enter down-current and drift back to the entry.

## Hazards

* **Steep slope.** The bottom falls away quickly and continues past 40 m, and it is easy to drift deep without noticing while following the reef. Watch depth and gas.
* **Entanglement.** The reef is man-made junk: tires, pipe, boats, line and appliances all snag. Carry a knife.
* **Current in the shallows.** Usually mild, but it runs stronger in the shallows than at depth and picks up toward the point and on large tidal exchanges.
* **Boat traffic.** Salmon fishing boats work the point in summer and small boats pass close. Fly a marker buoy and listen before ascending.
* **Access and neighbours.** Private property on both sides of the access, dawn-to-dusk hours with a posted fine, and a quiet residential street. Keep noise down, especially on night dives.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces north to northwest across East Passage.
* **Bad.** North through northwest, onshore with fetch up the passage, which puts chop on the entry.
* **Fine.** South, southeast and southwest are offshore, blowing off the point and the land behind the beach.
* **Wind against current.** The ebb sets north-northwest, so a north wind opposing it steepens the surface chop. That is the combination to avoid.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Often unremarkable by day and better at night, when the reef animals are out. A recent summer report put it around 6 m.
* **Blooms.** Spring and summer plankton blooms are the usual spoiler.
* **Silt.** The sand and silt bottom stirs easily, and wind chop clouds the shallows, so careful finning keeps the water clear.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 9 °C in winter and spring, up to about 14 °C in late summer. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the mid teens near the surface in summer.

## Marine life

* **Character.** The appeal is an artificial reef dropped onto an otherwise plain sand slope: the tires, pipe, boats and junk concentrate fish and invertebrates, while the surrounding sand carries its own quieter community of burrowers. Slow down and look small; the site rewards patience more than distance covered.
* **On the structure.** Tube worms and sea squirts crust the tires, and the sunken boats are cased in barnacles and small white anemones. Copper and juvenile black rockfish, lingcod that guard nests and eggs in late winter and spring, painted and kelp greenling and pile perch hold to the reef. Octopus are a highlight: the small Pacific red octopus turns up several times a dive, the giant Pacific octopus is an occasional find, and wolf eels are beginning to establish dens.
* **On the sand.** Moon snails plough the shallows for shellfish, tube-dwelling anemones stand down the slope with long white tentacles, and flounder and sole, including C-O sole, lie hidden until they bolt. Orange sea pens, sponges, reddish sea squirts and yellow social tunicates dot the bottom, with geoduck and burrowing anemones set into it, and blackeye and Bering hermit crabs, sharp-nosed and helmet crabs, chitons, shrimp and nudibranchs, among them the white-lined Dirona, working across it. Ratfish cruise the deeper soft ground in numbers.
* **Sea stars.** A diverse cast: blood, vermilion, pink, rose, morning sun, Stimpson's sun and sunflower stars work the reef and sand.
* **Sculpins and small fish.** Grunt, sailfin, great, Pacific staghorn and roughback sculpins, gunnels and snake pricklebacks along the cobble and sand. Stubby and opalescent squid appear in winter, and Pacific spiny lumpsuckers turn up in the kelp in late fall and winter.
* **Seasonal visitors.** Big skate and longnose skate move over the deeper sand below about 25 m, and six-gill sharks are a rare summer sighting, reported between roughly 10 and 35 m from June to October.
