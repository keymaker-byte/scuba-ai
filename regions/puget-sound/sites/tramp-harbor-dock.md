# Tramp Harbor Dock

Shore dive beside a former oil dock turned public fishing pier on the east side of Vashon Island, in Tramp Harbor off East Passage. A flat sand and lettuce kelp bottom runs out under the dock and slopes gradually away past its end, with plumose anemones on the pilings and a bottle garden off the southeast side that hides small red octopus. The harbor is sheltered and its tidal current is negligible, which makes it an easy, shallow site suited to every level and a favourite night dive. The headline hazard is silt: the fine bottom clouds at the first touch of a fin.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Climb down the bulkhead beside the dock to the beach and walk a few paces into the water. At a high tide the water is close to the bulkhead; on a minus tide the inner part of the dock dries and the walk across the sand is much longer. Snorkel out along the right-hand (south) side of the dock before descending.
* **Parking.** Free, in front of the bulkhead and picnic area, room for only four or five cars.
* **Access.** The dock itself has been closed to the public since 2019 for structural deterioration, and its ladder about three quarters of the way out is no longer usable; all diving is from the beach. The Vashon Park District plans to replace it with a narrower, slightly shorter dock, so expect construction and changes at the site once rebuilding begins. From the Fauntleroy ferry at the north end of the island or the Tahlequah ferry at the south end, the site is about 20 minutes' drive: take Vashon Highway SW to SW 204th Street, turn east, follow it as it becomes SW Ellisport Road and curves downhill to the water, then turn right onto Dockton Road SW to the dock.
* **Facilities.** A picnic table beside the dock.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The dock.** The pilings run about 100 m straight out from the beach, east-northeast, a built-in guide out and back. Plumose anemones cover them.
* **The bottle garden.** From the pilings about three quarters of the way out, where the bottom is around 6 m below MLLW, take a compass bearing on Robinson Point, the low point on the southeast skyline, and swim 10 to 30 kicks on it. Old bottles lie scattered across the sand, many of them occupied.
* **The slope.** Past the dock end the flat sand steepens gradually toward the middle of the harbor and keeps going past 20 m. Most of the life is on the shallower sand and around the dock.

* **Flood.** Sets south down East Passage at the station, with daily peaks of 0.19 to 0.42 m/s there over 30 days. Inside Tramp Harbor the tidal current is only a few centimetres per second.
* **Ebb.** Sets north-northwest up East Passage at the station, with daily peaks of 0.12 to 0.25 m/s there over the same 30 days. The harbor stays essentially still.
* **Diveable window.** Any time of the tide. The harbor sits out of the main flow of East Passage, so its weak current has no meaningful slack to time; the station gives only the state of the open passage beyond the harbor mouth, where current stays under 0.25 m/s for about 1 to 18 hours at a time.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | east-northeast, 73° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | east-northeast, 71° |

The beach faces east-northeast across Tramp Harbor into East Passage, with the island and Maury Island sheltering it from the west and south.

* **Bad.** North through east-southeast (N-ESE). North winds blow down the length of East Passage straight into the harbor over the longest fetch, building chop on the entry and the surface swim; northeast through east-southeast cross East Passage from the Des Moines shore, onshore over 5.8 to 8.9 km.
* **Short fetch.** Southeast (SE), onshore over 2.1 km.
* **Fine.** South-southeast clockwise through north-northwest (SSE-NNW), offshore and blocked by the island, or cross-shore over 1.3 km or less; south winds are largely blocked by Maury Island.
* **Wind against current.** Inside the harbor the current is too weak to stack against a wind. Out in East Passage, a north wind against the north setting ebb stacks up steep chop at the harbor mouth.

## Visibility

* **Typical.** Usually around 4.5 to 6 m, sometimes better in winter. Best on an outgoing tide.
* **Seasonal.** Follows the Sound's pattern, worst through the spring and summer plankton blooms. Shellfish spawning in late winter, geoducks and gaper clams, can cut the visibility sharply within a single dive.
* **Bottom.** The silt is the main local factor; a careless group can ruin the visibility for the swim back.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 7 to 11°C through the year, about 8°C in late winter. Standard drysuit protocol year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the low teens late in summer, warmest over the shallow sand along the dock.

## Marine life

* **Bottles.** Red octopus (Octopus rubescens), small enough to fit through a bottle's neck and often found inside them, plus the occasional giant Pacific octopus.
* **Pilings.** Plumose anemones, and orange plated tunicates tucked into the concrete blocks under the dock.
* **Sand and kelp.** Lettuce kelp across the flat bottom, with lion nudibranchs on the backs of the blades in summer. Dungeness, graceful, red rock, helmet and northern kelp crabs, Bering and blackeyed hermit crabs, shrimp, sea fleas and isopods. Geoducks and fat gaper clams, which spawn in late winter.
* **Nudibranchs.** Heath's dorid, barnacle-eating dorid, red-gilled and three-lined nudibranchs, white-lined dirona, Tritonia festiva, and orange and white nudibranchs.
* **Fish.** Spotted ratfish in water as shallow as 4 m, longnose skates, Pacific spiny lumpsuckers, snailfish, bay pipefish, shiner perch and tubesnouts.
* **Squid.** A winter breeding ground for stubby squid.

## Hazards

* **Silt.** The fine bottom stirs instantly. Weight for a shallow dive, stay off the bottom, and handle bottles gently.
* **Lion's mane jellies.** Common in the water column. If you have seen them, check for stinging tentacles before removing mask and gloves.
* **Shallow depth.** Much of the dive is in 3 to 10 m. Weight to stay down in the shallows and watch buoyancy on the ascent.
* **Depth creep.** The slope past the dock end keeps dropping past 20 m and is easy to follow deeper than planned on a flat, featureless bottom.
* **The closed dock.** The structure is deteriorating; stay off the deck and clear of anything loose around the pilings.
