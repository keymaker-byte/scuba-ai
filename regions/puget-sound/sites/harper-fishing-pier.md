# Harper Fishing Pier (Barbara G)

Shore dive off the Port of Bremerton's public fishing pier at Harper, on Yukon Harbor between Port Orchard and Southworth, looking across to Blake Island. A gentle silt and sand slope runs out past the pier end to the wreck of the Barbara G, a wooden fishing trawler lost in the Inauguration Day storm of 1993, with a small fiberglass boat hull beside it, broken stumps of the old pier and its dolphins, and a field of old bottles and bricks from the site's ferry landing and brickworks days. The current is negligible on almost every exchange, so it dives as an easy, shallow site at any stage of the tide. Fishing line, hooks and lost crab gear around the pier are the headline hazard.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Two ways in. Walk the pier about 135 m to the lower float at its end and giant stride off into about 4 m below MLLW, which puts you straight over the dive; or climb down the seawall of rock and stone steps at the base of the pier, about 2 m high, to the beach and walk in. Either way the exit is up the rocks and seawall at the pier base, a stiff climb in a drysuit, worst at low tide when the water's edge is furthest down the beach.
* **Parking.** Free. The lot beside the head of the pier takes only four or five cars; keep clear of the residence across the lot. Otherwise park along SE Southworth Drive and walk down.
* **Access.** A public pier owned and maintained by the Port of Bremerton, at 9969 SE Southworth Drive, Port Orchard, open to fishing, crabbing, squidding and diving. Hours are posted on the pier and have run 07:00 to 22:00. From the Fauntleroy to Southworth ferry, go straight about 1 km on SE Southworth Drive, turn right to stay on it, and follow it along the waterfront about 1 km more; the pier is at the sharp corner where the road turns south along the water. From Port Orchard, follow Bay Street into Beach Drive past Manchester State Park, turn left onto Main Street in Manchester and right onto Colchester Drive, which becomes Southworth Drive, about 6 km to Harper.
* **Facilities.** The pier and its float. No restrooms.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The pier.** The present pier opened in 2015 on the line of the historic ferry and Mosquito Fleet landing, running east-northeast from the road to a lower float at its end. Its pilings carry plumose anemones and shelter perch and tubesnouts.
* **Old pier stumps.** The old pier's pilings and dolphins were cut off near the bottom when they were removed, leaving a reef of broken stumps covered in plumose anemones, mostly off the southeast side of the pier end. The shallow inshore stretch holds no trace of them.
* **Barbara G.** A wooden fishing trawler about 20 m long, lying in about 7 m below MLLW roughly 100 to 140 m offshore, north-northeast of the pier end. The mast, crow's nest, rigging, a large steel fuel tank and a toilet are still recognisable, and parts stand up to 3 m off the bottom, but the hull is collapsing and the stern has caved in. A guide line runs along the bottom from the pier out to the wreck; summer algae and kelp often bury it. Without the line, a heading of 55° magnetic from the light pole at the head of the pier runs to the wreck.
* **Small wreck.** The fiberglass hull of a 5 to 6 m boat lies a short kick from the Barbara G, slightly deeper, joined to it by a short line.
* **Bottle and brick field.** Old bottles, jars and bricks from the landing and the brickworks that once stood here are scattered around the pier and its old dolphin sites, thickest out around 12 m below MLLW off the pier end.

| **Time offset** | Not established; slack should be confirmed in the water |

* **Flood.** At the pier the flood sets weakly southeast along the shore of Yukon Harbor, about 125°, while the station out in the channel sets southwest.
* **Ebb.** Sets weakly northwest along the shore, about 305°. A noticeable northward set along the bottom turns up occasionally on a big ebb; it is manageable by staying low and pulling along the bottom.
* **Diveable window.** The site is not a slack dive. While the station reads under 0.36 m/s there is no current at the pier, and up to about 0.51 m/s there is only a light current that still dives comfortably. The station's ebb dominates and its floods are often barely measurable, so on most days that covers most of the day. Check the station's peak for the day and save the dive for another day only when it runs well past 0.51 m/s.

## Depth and tide

The site is shallow enough that the tide changes its character: at a big low the pier end is barely 3 m deep, at a high it is over 8 m.

## Hazards

* **Fishing line and gear.** The pier is fished and crabbed hard. Monofilament, hooks, squid jigs, snagged crab pots and their lines litter the bottom around the pier and along the line to the wreck, and anglers cast from the pier overhead. Carry a cutting tool and keep clear of the water directly under active lines.
* **Fragile wreck.** The Barbara G's wooden hull is rotting and partly collapsed. Stay outside it and off it.
* **Guide lines.** The lines between the pier and the wrecks are partly buried and overgrown, and lie across the bottom where they catch fins and gear.
* **Boat traffic.** Small boats and crabbers work Yukon Harbor off the pier. Fly a dive flag and ascend near the pier or the line.
* **Entry and exit.** The seawall and rocks at the pier base are steep, uneven and slippery, and the climb out is the hardest part of the dive.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces east-northeast across Yukon Harbor toward Blake Island, with the harbor opening northeast onto the main channel.
* **Bad.** North through east winds blow straight in across the harbor and the open channel past Blake Island, building chop on the float entry and the rocky exit.
* **Fine.** South and west winds, Puget Sound's usual storm direction, blow offshore from the land behind the beach and leave the harbor calm.
* **Wind against current.** The current here is too weak for the combination to matter much; a northwest wind against a strong ebb is the only one worth noting.

## Visibility

* **Typical.** Usually 3 to 6 m, reaching 9 m and more on the best days, generally better on an ebb than on a flood.
* **Seasonal.** Follows the Sound's pattern, worst through spring and summer plankton blooms, best from late autumn through winter. In summer, sea lettuce and drifting algae cloud the shallows and mat the bottom.
* **Bottom.** The fine silt stirs easily, and the shallows near shore run murkier than the water out at the pier end and the wrecks.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 8°C in late winter and early spring, rising to 10 to 12°C by late summer. Standard drysuit protocol year round.
* **Surface layer.** The site is shallow enough that the whole dive sits close to the surface layer; there is rarely a distinct thermocline.

## Marine life

* **Wrecks.** Great sculpin, scalyhead sculpin, brown and copper rockfish, kelp and painted greenling, cabezon, and schools of pile perch and striped seaperch around the Barbara G, with juvenile wolf eels in the wreck's pipes. Giant Pacific octopus den occasionally under the small wreck and among the debris.
* **Pilings and stumps.** Giant plumose anemones, dock shrimp, stiletto shrimp and three-lined shrimp, with dense schools of tubesnouts and pile and shiner perch around the pier.
* **Nudibranchs.** Hooded nudibranchs (Melibe), shaggy mouse, frosted, British Columbia aeolids, three-lined aeolids, opalescent and thick-horned nudibranchs (Hermissenda), white-lined dirona, sea lemons, Nanaimo dorids, Heath's and Monterey dorids, and northern leopard dorids.
* **Bottom.** Red rock, Dungeness, helmet and northern kelp crabs, hermit crabs, moon snails, and white-spotted greenling and gunnels in the debris. Spiny dogfish and harbor seals pass through.
* **Water column.** Moon jellies, lion's mane jellies, and small red sausage and aggregating jellies carrying hitchhiking shrimp.
