# Saltwater State Park

Shore dive at Saltwater State Park in Des Moines, on the mainland shore of East Passage in Puget Sound, across from Vashon Island. The draw is an artificial reef of boulders and concrete pilings, in three parallel fingers running out from a decayed old barge wreck, all linked by guide lines and thick with rockfish, lingcod, wolf eels and octopus. The tradeoff for the reef is a long, tide dependent surface swim over an exposed sand and cobble flat, and a bottom that drops away sharply once past the reef. The whole area around the reef fingers is a marine protected area, no fishing or collecting.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Down the concrete stairs at the north end of the park's sandy swimming beach, entering the water near the south end of the jetty where McSorley Creek drains into the Sound. Swim out on the surface, roughly west, to conserve air, to a buoy that marks the remains of the old barge and the start of the dive.
* **Parking.** The lower parking lot, a short walk from the beach. A Washington Discover Pass is required, a one day pass or an annual pass, sold at the park and at retailers statewide; there is no separate per-vehicle gate fee.
* **Access.** Day use hours are 8 a.m. to dusk, year round. The campground is currently closed for erosion and flood related work in the lower camping area; check the park's status before planning an overnight stay.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms, hot and cold showers near the lower parking lot, picnic tables and reservable shelters, a playground, a nature trail and a seasonal concession stand (Memorial Day through Labor Day, weekends in spring and early fall). Nearest town is Des Moines.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The buoy and the wreck.** The buoy roughly 250 to 300 m offshore marks a wooden barge sunk here decades ago. It has mostly disintegrated into the sand; what is left is scattered timber and a few rusted spikes rather than a recognizable hull.
* **Guide lines.** From the buoy area, lines run north to south and east to west. The east to west line is the one that leads out to the reef.
* **The three reef fingers.** Built in 2009 from boulders and concrete pilings, replacing a 1970s tire reef that was fully removed by 2008 after research found tires were poor habitat and leached toxins. The fingers run roughly perpendicular to shore, spaced about 30 m apart, starting around 7 to 8 m below MLLW and sloping down to about 20 to 24 m before the bottom drops away more steeply into deeper water.
* **Eelgrass.** Inshore of the reef, where the sandy cobble shallows give way to clean sand, an eelgrass bed grows.
* **Old tire debris.** A larger field of tire debris from the original reef remains scattered further out and to the north of the maintained dive area. It is not part of the marked route and is easy to wander into by accident; stick to the guide lines.

| **Ebb axis** | north-northwest, about 342° |

* **Flood.** Sets south-southeast, parallel to the beach.
* **Ebb.** Sets north-northwest, also parallel to the beach.
* **Diveable window.** Current here is generally light, and local divers describe it as weak and variable rather than a clean, predictable exchange; it is not well represented by the standard south Sound approach of correcting off the Tacoma Narrows. Rather than chase a precise slack at a distant station, the established local practice is to plan around high water on a small exchange day: enter roughly 45 minutes to an hour before high water and exit about 30 minutes after. Avoid large exchanges, and avoid a strong ebb altogether if a southwest wind is blowing. Low water shortens the remaining swim once you reach the water, but only after a long, exposed walk across the drained tide flat to get there, and the walking is the worse trade.

## Depth and tide

East Passage swings up to about 5 m between high and low water here, and combined with the long entry flat, the tide governs this dive as much through the walk and swim as through the reef's own depth.

## Hazards

* **Long, tide dependent approach.** Up to about 270 m of surface swim each way at high tide. At low tide the swim itself shortens, but only because the flat has drained, so it is replaced by a long, hard walk over exposed sand and cobble before the water is even reached. Rock soled boots help.
* **Drop off past the reef.** The bottom falls away sharply beyond the outer end of the fingers, into water well past sport diving depths. It is easy to follow the structure deeper than intended.
* **Entanglement.** The guide line system, the reef pilings, and the old tire debris scattered beyond the marked route all snag. Carry a cutting tool.
* **Current with a southwest wind.** A southwest wind builds a stronger, rougher ebb, setting north-northwest against the wind. Avoid diving on an ebb under these conditions.
* **Creek outflow.** The entry sits at the mouth of McSorley Creek; runoff after heavy rain can cloud the shallows and add a local current near the outflow.
* **No collection.** The reef and its surroundings are a marine protected area. No fishing or harvesting.
* **Boat traffic.** Fly a marker buoy on the surface swim and while ascending.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces roughly west to southwest across East Passage toward Vashon Island.
* **Bad.** Southwest, which puts fetch and chop on the swim and, as above, drives a stronger and rougher ebb.
* **Fine.** East to northeast, off the wooded bluff behind the park.
* **Wind against current.** A southwest wind reinforcing the north-northwest ebb is the combination to avoid; it is the one this site's own divers warn about most.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Better at depth on the reef than in the shallows, which stay murkier even on a good day. Best on an outgoing tide, once creek runoff has cleared and the flat has stopped stirring. Drops after rain and during the spring and summer plankton blooms typical of the Sound.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 7 to 11 C through the year, standard for Puget Sound. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the low teens late in summer.

## Marine life

* **On the reef.** Wolf eels denning in the boulders and pilings, lingcod of real size, copper, quillback, black and vermilion rockfish, giant Pacific octopus, kelp and painted greenling, gunnels, warbonnets, plumose anemones in white and orange, nudibranchs, sea stars, urchins and scallops. Ratfish cruise the sand around the structure.
* **On the sand and in the eelgrass.** Moon snails, sea pens, geoduck, perch, flounder and sole including speckled sanddabs, hermit and kelp crabs, sea cucumbers, and hairy and stalked sea squirts on any hard surface they can find.
