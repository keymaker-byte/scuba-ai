# Warren Avenue Bridge

Warren Avenue Bridge crosses Port Washington Narrows in Bremerton, the tidal cut joining Dyes Inlet to Sinclair Inlet, where the current runs fast enough to keep the bottom scoured clean of silt. The dive works the concrete pylons supporting the bridge, each thick with plumose anemones, feather duster worms and giant barnacles. It is a slack tide dive; the current reverses within minutes of the published slack and builds quickly on either side of it.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** A short trail runs from the end of Elizabeth Avenue down to the beach, entering the water directly beneath the bridge. Swim out to the first pylon, then work out to the second and third.
* **Parking.** Roadside at the end of Elizabeth Avenue, space for a handful of cars. This is a residential street; leave driveways clear and carry out everything brought in.
* **Access.** Shore access is available 24 hours a day. The bridge carries Warren Avenue (SR 303) across the narrows, about 1.6 km west northwest of Point Herron.
* **Facilities.** None.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Bridge pylons.** Three concrete pylons support the span, stepping from shallow near shore to deeper toward the channel. Each has a concrete ledge at its base, fully exposed on the two outer pylons and still part of the buried structure at the first.
* **The bridge overhead.** On a bright day with decent visibility, the shimmering outline of the bridge above is enough to steer between the pylons. In lower visibility, carry a compass to hold a line between them.
* **Boulders at the outer pylons.** Boulders piled around the bases of the second and third pylons hold fish and invertebrates, and schools of small perch often cloud the water around them.

* **Flood.** Sets west-northwest, running up the narrows toward Dyes Inlet, with daily peaks of 1.05 to 1.76 m/s at the station over 30 days.
* **Ebb.** Sets east-southeast, running down the narrows toward Sinclair Inlet and out to Puget Sound through Rich Passage, with daily peaks of 0.52 to 1.48 m/s at the station over the same 30 days.
* **Diveable window.** The current becomes noticeable again within five to ten minutes of slack, so the window closes fast on either side of it. Its width varies a great deal by exchange, from under thirty minutes on a tight one to well over an hour and a half on a wide one; a typical window under 0.25 m/s at the station runs about 35 to 85 minutes. Begin the dive thirty to forty minutes ahead of the predicted slack, and start the swim back to shore as soon as the current turns.

A narrow inland cut through residential Bremerton, with land close on both sides.

* **Fine.** All directions (N-NNW). The surrounding shore leaves little open water for wind to build a fetch across, no more than 1.5 km from any direction.
* **Wind against current.** West-northwest (WNW) blows against the flood, over too short a fetch to matter; the current, not wind, sets the go or no go call.

## Depth and tide

The dive area sits in shallow water relative to the tidal range here, so the swing between a big low and a big high changes the working depth by close to half again: an 8 m point at MLLW can read under 7 m on a big low and over 11 m on a big high.

## Visibility

* **Current driven.** Best at slack, worsening as the current builds and stirs sediment off the bottom. Typical slack visibility runs 6 to 9 m, clearest on the longest, most generous slacks.
* **Thick fouling.** Dense feather duster worm and bryozoan growth on the pylons can obscure the structure itself even on a clear-water day.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 7 to 11 °C year round, the regional figure for Puget Sound. Standard drysuit protocol.

## Marine life

* **Pylons and hard structure.** Plumose anemones in white, orange, yellow green and cream, giant acorn barnacles, yellow and orange sponge, and dense clumps of feather duster tube worms thick enough to obscure the bridge piers beneath them. Red brown sea squirts and, increasingly, invasive bryozoans also cover the structure.
* **Clay bottom.** Patches of hard clay are riddled with piddock clam burrows, the clams' necks often visible extending above the rim. Small sculpins and gunnels shelter in the empty holes.
* **Fish.** Red Irish lords, pile perch and other perch, flounder, sailfin sculpins and kelp greenlings, with schools of small perch clouding the water around the outer pylons.
* **Bottom and sea stars.** Sandy cobble near shore, grading to a mix of cobble and clay offshore. Morning sun stars, Stimpson's sun stars, sunflower sea stars, pink sea stars and blood stars are scattered across it, along with snails, nudibranchs and crabs. Shallow water holds colonies of small aggregating anemones.

## Hazards

* **Strong, fast reversing current.** The defining hazard. Current direction changes rapidly after slack, and a diver who lingers past the turn is caught working against it on the swim back.
* **Boat traffic.** The narrows carries continuous small boat traffic between Dyes Inlet and Sinclair Inlet, directly overhead of the dive.
