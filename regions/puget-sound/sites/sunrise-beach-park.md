# Sunrise Beach Park

Shore dive at Sunrise Beach Park, north of Gig Harbor on the west shore of Colvos Passage, a few kilometres up from the Tacoma Narrows. The draw is a hard rock wall running parallel to shore, undercut with ledges, fissures and crevices that hold resident wolf eels and giant Pacific octopus in numbers few sites in the Sound can match, with a boulder slope falling away below it into deep water. The wall sits inside the Colvos Passage Marine Preserve. Its headline hazard is the current: Colvos Passage sets north past the beach on most of the tidal cycle, the usable window is short and comes before the slack at the Narrows, and a long surface swim and a steep hike back up to the car leave little margin for a mistimed dive.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the lot, a graded footpath of about 250 paces drops steeply to a log retaining wall, a shed and a small cobble beach. Enter in front of the trail and swim south along shore, past a cabin and two houses, to a deformed evergreen tree that leans out over the water. The north end of the wall lies offshore of it.
* **Parking.** The unpaved lot at the park, at the end of a long, steep hill on Sunrise Beach Drive NW. Park where directed.
* **Access.** Open 7 a.m. to dusk. The gate closes at dusk, so be out of the water and have the car out of the park before then. The trail is steep and slippery when wet, and a fallen tree has partly obstructed it near the beach; expect to step over or around debris. The beach south of the park boundary, marked by a post on the beach, is private; stay in the water when passing it. The site is also dived by boat from Gig Harbor and Point Defiance, using the dive mooring buoys at the reef.
* **Facilities.** Portable restrooms and picnic tables by the parking lot. No showers, no water.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The surface swim.** About 190 m from the entry to the north end of the wall, south along the beach past a cabin and two houses. Swimming in the shallows until close to the site saves air and keeps the swim against any current short.
* **The deformed tree.** A gnarled evergreen growing out at an angle over the water marks where to drop. From the tree, a compass course of 115° descends across sand and, in summer, through bull kelp to the top centre of the wall's north end.
* **The sand slope.** Gentle sand runs from the beach out to the top of the wall, with no handholds. In summer the top of the wall is marked by bull kelp, which is the last thing to hold on the way back in.
* **The wall.** Hard rock, roughly 6 m high and about 70 m long, running south parallel to shore, riddled with undercut ledges, fissures and crevices. The dens of the resident wolf eels lie at both ends; the giant Pacific octopus favour the deeper cracks.
* **The south end.** The southern section of the wall has collapsed into a boulder and rubble field; the fissure behind its old face is gone.
* **Below the wall.** Collapsed rock and boulders cascade from the base at about 15 m down the slope past 30 m, fading into sand around 30 m and beyond.
* **Mooring buoys.** Two dive mooring buoys mark the north and south ends of the reef, and their lines make a convenient descent and ascent.

## Current

| | |
|---|---|
| **Governing station** | NOAA The Narrows, 0.3 miles North of Bridge (PUG1527), about 8 km south |
| **Recommended bin** | bin 19, at 7 m, nearest the top of the wall; bin 12 at 20.9 m slacks within about 10 minutes of it |
| **Time offset** | Slack before flood about −1:15 to the station's slack before flood; before ebb the current only slows |
| **Flood axis** | south-southwest, about 198° |
| **Ebb axis** | northeast, about 40° |

* **Flood.** Locally the current runs parallel to shore, along 27° and 207°. As the Narrows begins to flood, the water at the site sets south past the entry and the wall for a short period, then goes slack again a few minutes after the station turns to flood and swings north. For most of the flood it runs north past the beach, back toward the entry.
* **Ebb.** Sets north along the shore, toward the entry. Colvos Passage carries a net northward flow, so the ebb simply continues what the late flood started. Before ebb the north going current slows, around 50 minutes ahead of the station's slack before ebb, then builds again on the ebb, often without ever stopping.
* **Diveable window.** The slack before flood, on a small exchange. The site turns about 1:15 ahead of the station's slack before flood, so plan to be on the wall from around then. For a dive that rides the current both ways, enter about 30 minutes before the station's slack before flood: the water is then setting south, which carries a diver along the beach to the wall, and it reverses to north shortly after the station begins to flood, carrying the diver back toward the entry. The end of the ebb, just before this window, is the hardest part of the cycle to predict, with weak flow fluctuating in direction and the possibility of downcurrents; do not drop early into it. Exchanges of about 2.5 m or less at the Gig Harbor tide station give the longest windows; a big exchange can leave current running through the whole dive. On a low exchange day the slowing before ebb is also diveable, as a short lull.

## Depth and tide

The tide swings up to about 5 m here, so the top of the wall can read anywhere from about 7 m to 13 m on the gauge.

## Hazards

* **Current.** Strong and variable off the window, and set along shore toward the north on most of the cycle. Diving late leaves a swim back against it; diving the end of the ebb risks flow that changes direction and downcurrents along the wall.
* **Long swim and steep hike.** About 190 m of surface swim each way, then a steep climb back to the car in full gear. Take the walk up slowly after a hard swim, since heavy exertion after a dive raises the risk of decompression sickness.
* **Depth below the wall.** The boulder slope keeps going well past 30 m, and the wolf eel and octopus dens draw divers down it. Watch the computer.
* **Boat traffic.** Fishing boats and dive charters use the reef, and charters anchor or moor on it. Listen for engines, fly a surface marker, and make the safety stop in the kelp, then stay close to the bottom on the swim back to shore.
* **Bull kelp.** Entanglement in summer over the top of the wall. Carry a cutting tool.
* **Lion's mane jellies.** Seasonal in the water column. Check for tentacles on hoods and gloves before removing a mask.
* **Wildlife.** The wolf eels and octopus here are accustomed to divers but are wild animals. Do not feed, handle or restrain them; a wolf eel's bite is serious.
* **No collection.** The reef lies inside the Colvos Passage Marine Preserve. Taking shellfish, bottomfish or any other marine life is prohibited.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces east-southeast across Colvos Passage toward Vashon Island, about 2 km away.
* **Bad.** Southerlies, which come up Colvos Passage from Dalco Passage with fetch behind them and put chop on the surface swim, and northerlies down the length of Colvos Passage, which build a short steep chop against the beach. A strong surface current blown up by the wind can run when the water at depth is mild.
* **Fine.** West, offshore off the wooded bluff behind the park. Easterlies are short fetched across the passage and usually minor.
* **Wind against current.** A southerly against the north going current along shore is the combination to avoid; it makes the swim back to the entry rough and slow.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Usually 3 to 6 m on the wall, reaching 9 m and more on a clear day, and noticeably worse in the shallows and the top few metres. Best from late summer through winter, worst in the spring and summer plankton blooms. Visibility drops as the current picks up, so it is usually best early in the window.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 8 C in late winter to about 12 C in autumn. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** The top layer runs colder than depth in winter and warms into the low teens late in summer.

## Marine life

* **On the wall.** Resident wolf eels, including mated pairs and juveniles, and giant Pacific octopus in the dens, with red octopus in the smaller cracks. Mosshead warbonnets in vivid red and white, red Irish lords (often on eggs in winter), buffalo, grunt, great, sailfin and scalyhead sculpins, painted and kelp greenlings, lingcod guarding egg masses in winter, copper and brown rockfish, gunnels, and occasional red brotula and lumpsuckers. The rock carries pink encrusting sponge, swimming pink scallops, barnacles thick with yellow sponge, purple plume worms, red slipper cucumbers, white and orange sea cucumbers and small colorful anemones.
* **Invertebrates throughout.** Leather, blood, mottled, morning sun and sunflower stars, green urchins, chitons including gumboots, kelp and decorator crabs, heart crabs, and many nudibranchs: lemon peel and Nanaimo dorids, diamondback, frosted and clown nudibranchs, red dendronotids and three lined aeolids.
* **On the sand and in the shallows.** Orange sea pens, hermit crabs, moon snails, flounder and C-O soles, blackeye gobies, seaperch, ratfish and dogfish, with schools of shiner, pile and striped perch over the reef. Squid lay eggs here in winter.
* **Passing through.** California sea lions and harbor seals, especially in the colder months.
