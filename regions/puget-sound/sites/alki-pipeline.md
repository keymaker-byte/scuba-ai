# Alki Pipeline

Shore dive on the southwest side of Alki Point in West Seattle, off Constellation Park at the south end of 63rd Avenue SW. The site is an old outfall pipeline, broken and encrusted, running as a narrow rock path southwest across clean white sand into the open main basin of Puget Sound. It is shallow, easy to reach, and dived by all levels, a common night dive, with the pipe itself acting as a guide rope out and back. The bottom lies inside the Constellation Park Marine Reserve, where taking marine life is prohibited. The headline hazards are surf on a southwest-facing beach and the shallow, exposed profile.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the beach in front of the concrete pump station at Constellation Park, at the south end of 63rd Avenue SW where it meets Beach Drive SW. Walk down the gently sloping concrete ramp toward the pump station, then over the cobbled beach to the water, staying off the private beach property to the side.
* **Parking.** On the street along Beach Drive SW by Charles Richey Sr. Viewpoint and Constellation Park, in the 6300 block. Free, and it fills on summer weekends.
* **Access.** Under Seattle park hours, roughly 04:00 to 23:30. About 18 km from central Seattle over the West Seattle Bridge, then Harbor Avenue SW and Alki Avenue SW around the point to 63rd Avenue SW and Beach Drive SW.
* **Marine reserve.** The site sits in the Constellation Park Marine Reserve. Taking of fish, shellfish and seaweed is prohibited. Diving and observing are fine.
* **Facilities.** A portable toilet stands at the foot of the entry ramp by the pump station. More complete restrooms, a picnic beach and interpretive tidepool art are at Constellation Park, toward the north end of 63rd Avenue SW. Fast food nearby across the neighbourhood. Air fills are in Seattle, not on site.

## Navigation and landmarks

The pipeline is the dive: find it, follow it out, follow it back. Working from the entry:

* **Surface swim.** Snorkel out to the southwest, lining up with the wall of the pump station, for about 140 m until rocks appear on the bottom. Easier to do on a higher tide, when the entry is shorter and the cobble is covered.
* **Start of the reef.** The scattered rocks mark the beginning of the pipeline, a broken rock path 1 to 1.5 m wide laid over clean white sand.
* **Along the pipe.** Follow the rock path southwest as it descends gently across the sand. Orange and white plumose anemones tilt off the top of the structure the whole way out.
* **The apparent end.** A profusion of anemones marks the apparent end of the pipe, about 185 m offshore, at roughly 8 to 11 m below datum.
* **The true end.** The pipeline reappears and ends about 46 m farther out. To find it, hold a straight compass line southwest across the sand from the apparent end. This outermost section is the least visited part of the site and easy to miss.
* **Return.** The pipe is the guide back in. Follow it northeast to the shallows and the pump-station wall.

## Current

Currents here are light to moderate, and the site dives easily inside the point, but Alki Point sits just northwest and accelerates the flow close to and off the point, so a slack is worth planning. Local practice is to dive the slack before the ebb, entering the water about 45 minutes ahead of it.

|---|---|
| **Governing station** | NOAA Alki Point, West of (PUG1516), about 0.7 km northwest off the point |
| **Recommended bin** | bin 17, at 11.5 m, which matches the working depth. The deeper published bins (31 m, 59 m) sit in the channel, below anything dived here |
| **Time offset** | Not firmly established; the station sits off the point and the point speeds the flow nearby, so treat its slack as approximate and give it margin |
| **Flood axis** | south-southwest, 215° |
| **Ebb axis** | north, 4° |

* **Flood.** Sets south-southwest, down-sound past the point and into the main basin. Along the reef it runs southwest, in the direction the pipe leads.
* **Ebb.** Sets north, back around Alki Point toward Elliott Bay.
* **Diveable window.** Plan the slack before the ebb and splash about 45 minutes before it. The inshore ground over the pipe and sand carries little current on a small exchange; the livelier water is out and off the point.

## Depth and tide

The site is shallow the whole way, sloping gradually from the shallows at the reef start to about 8 to 11 m below datum at the apparent end of the pipe, and a metre or so deeper at the true end. The main basin swings 3 to 5 m between high and low water here, a large fraction of the site's total depth, so depths are given against the MLLW chart datum and a low tide reads noticeably shallower than a high.

## Hazards

* **Surf and exposure.** The beach faces southwest into open Puget Sound with a long fetch. A strong south wind blows up surf on the entry and can make getting in and out difficult.
* **Shallow profile.** The site is shallow throughout, so weight and buoyancy want care, and the surge from any swell reaches the whole dive.
* **Combined sewer overflow.** A wastewater pump station stands at the entry, and heavy rain can drive sewer overflows in the area. Dive after dry spells, not during or just after heavy rain.
* **Lion's mane jellies.** Common in the fall. If you have seen them, check your hood, mask and gloves for stinging tentacles before removing them.
* **Kelp.** Bull kelp historically formed a fringing canopy over this shoreline but has largely disappeared from it, in line with the wider Puget Sound decline, so expect little to no surface canopy. Broad-leaf kelp and encrusting growth remain on the pipe. Carry a knife regardless.
* **Boat traffic.** Small-boat traffic can be heavy in this shallow, popular water through the summer. Fly a marker buoy, and listen for engines before any final ascent.
* **Harbor seals.** Resident along this shore. Keep at least 90 m away; do not approach.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces southwest into the open main basin.
* **Bad.** South through west, onshore with open fetch down the Sound, which builds surf on the entry. A strong southerly is the classic dive-killer here.
* **Fine.** North, northeast and east are offshore, blowing off the West Seattle land behind the beach.
* **Wind against current.** A southerly opposing the southwest-setting flood steepens the chop on the entry. When a south wind is up, Seacrest Park, around the point in Elliott Bay and sheltered from it, is the standard fallback.

## Visibility

* **Recent form.** A recent report put the site at a clear 6 m or more. Visibility here is variable, though, and often more modest.
* **Blooms.** Spring and summer plankton blooms are the usual spoiler.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine white sand that stirs easily, so careful trim and finning keep the water clear, especially on the shallow swim in and out.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Around 12 °C in summer over the sand; roughly 7 to 14 °C across the year. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Because the site is shallow, the water warms more in summer than a deeper dive would, into the low to mid teens near the surface.

## Marine life

* **Character.** A pipeline and sand site rather than a wall: hard structure laid across an otherwise open sand plain, so the life concentrates on and immediately around the rock while the surrounding sand carries its own quieter community. The bottom is protected as a marine reserve, which is part of why it stays productive.
* **On the reef.** Orange and white plumose anemones tilt off the top of the pipe the length of the structure, thickest at the anemone profusion near the end. Pink swirls of tube worms, kelp crabs, sculpins, painted greenling, blood stars, sunflower stars and nudibranchs work the angular rocks.
* **On the sand.** Orange sea pens stand in fields off the pipe, with moon snails, flounders and tube-dwelling anemones scattered across the bottom around them. The flats away from the pipe carry eelgrass in places, and at night spiny dogfish and skates move over the open sand alongside the flounders.
* **Octopus.** The pipe once held caves and holes that sheltered a large octopus, though the caves are silting in. Giant Pacific octopus range throughout this shore.
* **Harbor seals.** Seen regularly along the point. Keep your distance, at least 90 m.
