# Keystone Jetty (Fort Casey)

Shore dive along the boulder jetty that shelters the Coupeville to Port Townsend ferry harbour, in Fort Casey State Park on the west side of Whidbey Island, facing Admiralty Bay. The jetty is a pile of large boulders built like a pyramid, with more rock below the surface than above, dropping to a sand bottom and encrusted with walls of white plumose anemones. It is a marine preserve, so nothing is collected. The current through Admiralty Inlet is the defining constraint, strong and erratic, and it never goes fully slack in one of its phases, so the site is planned from a current table rather than a tide table.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Enter the water on the east side of the jetty, submerge, and work along the large boulders. The jetty is built like a pyramid, with far more rock below the waterline than above. Divers with a dive-kayak can launch from the ramp and paddle the 200 m around the jetty rather than swim it, leaving the kayak on the far beach and entering on the east side. Do not swim that gap, as the current is strong and it crosses the ferry lane.
* **Parking.** In the Keystone State Park lot. A Washington Discover Pass or a day fee is required.
* **Access.** 08:00 to dusk. About 47 mi from Seattle, 66 mi from Bellingham.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms with wheelchair-accessible flush toilets and hot showers, an outside cold shower for rinsing gear, picnic tables, barbecue stands, a boat launch and ample parking on site. A restaurant across the street. Camping across the harbour at Fort Casey Historical State Park.

## Navigation and landmarks

The jetty is the spine of the dive, worked out along the boulders and back. Working from the beach:

* **The jetty.** A pyramid of large boulders running from the beach out into Admiralty Inlet, down to sand at the end. Hundreds of gaps between the rocks make hiding places, and the structure holds bull kelp and a heavy invertebrate cover. The first half suits snorkelers and divers of any level; the end of the jetty and the wharf commit you to more current and are best kept for experienced divers or done with a guide.
* **East face.** The sheltered harbour side of the jetty is the one that is entered and dived, and where the life is thickest.
* **Rock of Life.** A boulder along the jetty especially heavily encrusted with life, a useful landmark and a common turnaround for a conservative dive.
* **Octopus den.** A giant Pacific octopus has denned in the shallows at around 8 m below datum.
* **The end of the jetty.** Where the boulders run out into the current. The far end and the opposite (west) face can be explored when the current allows, but the flow is strongest here.
* **Wharf pilings.** About 230 m east of the jetty, a group of old wharf pilings stands over sand at roughly 6 to 11 m, covered in plumose anemones and feather duster worms, a separate and more sheltered dive. It tolerates the exchanges better than the jetty and can be drifted between the pilings and the jetty on a flood or an ebb, but current still runs here, so slack is the safe default, especially on the ebb.

## Current

Current is the defining constraint, strong and erratic, and it does not behave simply. Plan from a current table, not a tide table.

| **Current type** | Tidal |

| **Station flood axis** | southeast, 137° |
| **Station ebb axis** | north-northwest, 333° |

| **Site ebb set** | west-southwest, 253° |

* **Flood.** Sets southeast up Admiralty Inlet at the station, with daily peaks of 1.34 to 2.30 m/s over 30 days. Locally, the early flood runs in along the jetty, then turns in the shallows and flows parallel to shore toward the wharf pilings. A large exchange striking the jetty divides in two, one arm running out along the rocks and one inshore.
* **Ebb.** Sets north-northwest out of the inlet at the station, with daily peaks of 0.90 to 2.93 m/s over the same 30 days. Locally the flow runs southwest, past the wharf toward the jetty, then turns and flows out along the rocks at the jetty.
* **Diveable window.** The slack before flood only, with a typical window under 0.25 m/s at the station of about 25 to 55 minutes. True slack before ebb does not happen here, the current merely slows before building again, so it is not a window. The current can run hard at the exposed end even when the shore looks calm. The flow is slower on the bottom, the boulders give hand holds and shelter, and the rule in current is to swim with it or across it, never against it.
* **When the slack fails to arrive.** A slack at the station is necessary but not sufficient. The jetty current is a local eddy off the main channel, and on some exchanges the channel passes through slack while the eddy keeps running southwest and never truly stops. The pattern to watch for is a strongly diurnal day, or a flood following the slack that is much weaker than the ebb before it: the flood is what resets the flow, and a weak one may leave the current simply running on. Before committing, read the max flood and ebb either side of the slack rather than the slack alone. Prefer a slack flanked by two moderate, roughly equal maxes; be wary when the following flood is weak or the window is short, since the site then turns fast and being a few minutes late leaves you in current. Be ready to abort if it is still running at the end of the jetty.
* **Read the surface before committing.** The clock and the station prediction only get you close; confirm from the beach before gearing up. Floating logs and other debris drifting across the harbour show whether the water is actually moving and which way, a direct read on the eddy that the station's slack time cannot promise on its own.

The park faces Admiralty Bay, open to the south and southeast down Admiralty Inlet. The dived east face of the jetty is partly sheltered by the boulder pile.

* **Bad.** East-southeast through south (ESE-S), onshore over 5.5 to 14.2 km, southeast above all, which blows surf straight onto the exposed beach and ruins the entry. Do not dive it.
* **Short fetch.** East-northeast through east (ENE-E), onshore over 1.9 to 4.2 km.
* **Mixed.** South-southwest through southwest (SSW-SW), cross-shore over 6.7 to 12.3 km.
* **Fine.** West-southwest clockwise through northeast (WSW-NE): offshore from west-southwest through north, and cross-shore under 330 m from north-northeast and northeast.
* **Wind against current.** East-northeast through east-southeast (ENE-ESE), over 1.9 to 5.5 km, opposes the flood and builds steep wind-over-tide chop as the flood makes up, right on the slack-before-flood window you dive. Southwest (SW), over 12.3 km, opposes the ebb.

## Visibility

* **Best on the late flood and the slack before ebb.** Visibility is usually best during the last part of a flood exchange and as the current slows before the ebb, when cleaner water has filled in and the bottom has not yet been stirred.
* **Stirred easily.** Current, surge and ferry wash lift silt off the bottom, so away from the clean phases it can drop quickly.
* **Blooms.** Spring and summer plankton blooms are the usual seasonal spoiler.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 9 to 13 °C through the year, and around 12 °C in summer. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** In summer the surface runs warmer than the dive; dress for the depth.

## Marine life

* **Character.** A current-fed boulder reef in a protected marine preserve, so the rock is densely covered and the animals are unafraid. The fish are fat and notably tame after years of sanctuary protection, and divers routinely hand-feed the kelp greenlings beside the jetty, while the painted greenlings can be tame to the point of getting in a photographer's way. Walls of white plumose anemones drape the boulders like whipped cream, with cabezon and lingcod tucked among them and clouds of sand lances sweeping past overhead.
* **Fish.** Cabezon and lingcod on the structure, schools of rockfish and herring in the water column, kelp, whitespotted and painted greenlings, buffalo and scalyhead sculpins in the rock, striped seaperch, and tube-snouts under the kelp. Wolf eels and octopus turn up too, including a giant Pacific octopus denned in the shallows.
* **Anemones and encrusting life.** Giant plumose and painted anemones on the boulders, giant rock scallops cemented into the rock, giant acorn barnacles, green sea urchins, sea cucumbers, yellow social tunicates, chitons and limpets.
* **Nudibranchs.** A good site for them, in keeping with the Salish Sea's nudibranch diversity: clown, Nanaimo and Heath's dorid among the species seen.
* **Crustaceans.** Heart crabs, Bering hermit crabs, sharp-nosed crabs and shrimp work the rock.
* **At the wharf pilings.** The old pilings east of the jetty carry their own plumose anemones and dense clusters of purple and green feather duster worms, the largest tube worm in Puget Sound, and schools of seaperch drift between them.
* **Kelp.** Bull kelp grows on and around the jetty, best over the shallow boulders on a higher tide.

## Hazards

* **Current.** Erratic and strong, the headline hazard. It runs fast around the end of the jetty even during a nominal slack, and on a large exchange it splits and sets in more than one direction. Stay close to the boulders, use them for shelter and hand holds, and turn before the end rather than rounding it.
* **Ferry and boat traffic.** The jetty is the breakwater for the Coupeville to Port Townsend ferry, and there is a boat launch alongside. On the ferry side of the jetty, keep close to the rocks and well clear of the ferry lane and the launch.
* **Entanglement.** Broken fishing line and bull kelp snag on the boulders. Carry a knife, and check whether anyone is fishing from the jetty before you get in.
