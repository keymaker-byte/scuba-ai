# Richmond Beach Park (Richmond Beach Saltwater Park)

Shore dive on the main basin of Puget Sound at Shoreline, just north of Seattle, reached down a steep hill and across a pedestrian bridge over an active BNSF rail line. It is a casual, easygoing dive on a sand and cobble shelf that once served as a ship breaking ground, so the draw is a scattered field of debris, cinderblock, chain, concrete anchor blocks and a propeller, more than any natural reef. The shelf ends in a drop-off a short swim out, beyond which the debris field lies on a gentle slope. Its defining hazard is not the water at all but the railroad crossing itself, which has a history of pedestrian deaths and is why the bridge exists.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the beach directly in front of the restrooms and picnic shelter, at the bottom of the park's hill. Submerge and swim out; the cobble bottom turns to sand within the first few metres.
* **Crossing.** The park is split by a double BNSF main line along the shore. An elevated pedestrian bridge carries visitors and gear over the tracks; use it, never cross at grade. The corridor has a history of pedestrian fatalities, which is why the bridge and its locking gate exist.
* **Parking.** A large lot at the bottom of the hill, down a steep entrance drive from 20th Avenue NW, free of charge. It fills on warm weekends.
* **Access.** Open dawn to dusk daily, gated at night.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms and two reservable picnic shelters with tables and barbecue stands in the beach section, a playground and a reservable view terrace on the bluff above, unpaved trails, and a seasonal off leash dog area (November 1 to March 15).

## Navigation and landmarks

* **From the picnic shelter.** Head out on about 230°. A debris field begins around 3 m below MLLW and continues intermittently to about 7.5 m.
* **The drop.** Past the debris field the shelf falls away to a section of cinderblock and rope trail with a partly buried anchor chain at about 10.5 m below MLLW.
* **The deep field.** Turning to about 260°, the slope continues out to large concrete anchor blocks and more chain at about 14 to 15 m below MLLW, along with a large steel propeller on the order of 4 m across.
* **Return.** Come back on about 050°, which closes the loop toward the entry rather than retracing the outbound leg. A short side excursion to the southeast off the return track crosses another patch of metal debris. Safety stop over the shelf on the way in.
* **Substrate.** Sandy cobble in the shallows, sand only by about 0.5 to 1 m below MLLW, plain sand across most of the shelf and slope apart from the scattered debris itself.

## Current

| **Governing station** | NOAA President Point, 1.5 miles East of (PUG1511), about 3.3 km west, mid-channel |
| **Recommended bin** | bin 38, at 21.6 m, the shallowest published; no bin near the site's own working depth exists |
| **Time offset** | Not established; the station sits well out into the channel, so treat its slack as approximate and give it margin |
| **Flood axis** | south, about 177° |
| **Ebb axis** | north, about 3° |

* **Flood.** Sets south down the main basin channel, running roughly parallel to this stretch of shore.
* **Ebb.** Sets north up the channel, the reverse of the flood, also roughly shore parallel.
* **Current increases with depth.** Both the station and a spatial current model show this stretch of the main basin as generally weak. In the water it runs usually mild near shore but noticeably stronger below about 9 m, over the drop and across the outer debris field, and can run substantial on a larger exchange. Treat the model and station numbers as a floor, not a ceiling, and default to slack, especially for the outer, deeper part of the dive.
* **Diveable window.** Plan tight to the station slack, save the outer debris field for the calmest part of the tide, and be ready to shorten the excursion if current builds sooner than expected.

## Depth and tide

The shelf is shallow enough that tide is a large fraction of the working depth, and the debris field's outer edge only clears the water enough to be comfortably worked within a couple of hours of slack water on some tides.

## Hazards

* **The railroad crossing, not the dive.** The single biggest risk at this site has nothing to do with the water. Cross only on the pedestrian bridge, keep gear and children off the tracks at all times, and never take a shortcut at grade; trains on this line are fast and quiet enough to close distance without warning.
* **Current at depth.** Generally mild inshore, but builds below about 9 m and across the outer debris field, more than the station or model predictions suggest. Plan the deep, outer part of the dive for slack.
* **Entanglement.** The debris field includes rope, cable, chain and cinderblock, along with assorted metal wreckage. Carry a cutting tool.
* **Disorientation.** Most of the shelf is plain sand between debris patches, easy to swim across without crossing anything for want of a bearing. Run the dive on compass headings rather than by eye.
* **The hill.** A steep walk down from the parking lot to the beach, and the same walk back up in gear afterward. The path takes a wheeled cart; budget a rest before the climb.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces generally west across the main basin, open enough for a sightline to the Olympics on a clear day.
* **Bad.** West through southwest brings fetch straight up the main basin and chops the entry.
* **Fine.** East is offshore, off the bluff behind the park.
* **Wind against current.** The current here runs roughly north-south, parallel to the shore, so a west wind does not oppose either phase directly; it is the fetch itself, not a wind-against-current standoff, that spoils this entry.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Typically modest and can run murky, clearer at depth than in the silty shallows on a still day. Nothing about the site itself flushes it especially well or badly; check a recent report before driving out.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Consistent with the rest of the main basin, roughly 7 to 14 °C through the year depending on season. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Warms well above the dive in summer, as elsewhere in the main basin.

## Marine life

* **Fish.** Copper, brown and quillback rockfish, cabezon, grunt and great sculpins along with several unidentified small sculpins, white spotted greenling, tube-snouts, perch, flounder, C-O sole and other flatfish, spotted ratfish, and an occasional skate.
* **Invertebrates.** White plumose, stubby rose and tube dwelling anemones, the Columbia sand (sand rose) anemone, yellow sponges, solitary glassy and stalked sea squirts, spiny-headed and stalked hairy tunicates, nudibranchs, orange and California sea cucumbers, and yellow sea pens.
* **Sea stars.** Sand stars, spiny red and pink stars, mottled stars and sunflower stars work the sand and the debris.
* **Crustaceans.** Dungeness and red rock crabs, hermit crabs, and shrimp among the debris and along the sand.
* **On the wreckage.** The scattered ship breaking debris, cinderblock, chain and concrete anchor blocks, concentrates life the way it does at other artificial sites in the region and is the main reason to work the deeper part of the dive.
* **From the surface.** Harbor seals and harbor porpoises are seen off the beach regularly, and gray whales and orcas pass through the main basin here on occasion, generally a shore sighting rather than something encountered on the dive itself.
