# Lime Kiln Point

Shore dive below the Lime Kiln lighthouse in Lime Kiln Point State Park, on the west side of San Juan Island facing Haro Strait. Divers climb down a rocky cleft beside the lighthouse, duck under a bull kelp canopy, and descend a steep wall of ledges, cascading rock and boulders covered in pink coralline algae, sponges, hydrocoral and anemones, with wolf eels and giant Pacific octopus in its dens. The wall drops past 40 m within about 150 m of shore. Haro Strait's tidal stream sweeps the point, and the dive is timed to slack, best before a small exchange.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk down through the rocky cleft immediately south of the lighthouse, enter the water, and duck under the kelp canopy to begin the descent along the rock ledges. The rock is steep, slick and sharp, so enter only when the swell is small. A second protected cove on the north side of the lighthouse, near the whale watching platform, serves as an alternative entry and exit, so a dive can start at one and finish at the other with the current.
* **Parking.** The park's lower lot, beside the interpretive center. A Discover Pass is required: $10 for the day or $45 for the year, sold at the park's pay stations. The lot fills on summer weekends.
* **Access.** The park is open 8 a.m. to dusk year round. From the northwest end of the lot, walk the gated service road, which passes left of a wooden water tank and winds about 240 m downhill to the lighthouse; a cart or wheelbarrow helps with the gear, and the walk back is uphill. Reach the island on the Washington State Ferry from Anacortes to Friday Harbor, about 1 to 2 hours depending on stops. From Friday Harbor, follow Spring Street and San Juan Valley Road west, then Douglas Road and Bailer Hill Road, which becomes West Side Road and runs to the park entrance on a sharp curve. Boats are asked to stay about 800 m from the lighthouse under the voluntary whale protection zone along the island's west side. The park asks visitors to leave its marine animals in the water.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms, drinking water, picnic tables along the shoreline trails, the interpretive center (open daily in summer), the lighthouse (open to visitors Thursday to Sunday afternoons), and a seasonal snack stand open May through September.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The lighthouse.** The white 1919 lighthouse on its low rocky bluff marks the point from the water, with the entry cleft just south of it and the second cove just north.
* **Kelp shallows.** From the cleft, rock ledges run out under bull kelp in the shallows, a canopy that dims the light below it in summer. The small bay south of the lighthouse sits out of the main stream, a place to wait out the last of the current before slack, though the ebb eddies through it.
* **The wall.** Straight out from the lighthouse, past the kelp edge at the dive coordinate, rock ledges, small walls and cascading boulders drop steeply: about 19 m below MLLW 15 m further west, 32 m at 37 m out, and 38 m at 66 m out, continuing past 40 m. The best colour sits from about 18 m down.
* **North of the lighthouse.** The slope runs past 25 m within about 130 m north-northwest of the dive coordinate, with a small wall near the base of the dived slope running north from below the lighthouse, holding rockfish schools and wolf eel dens under its ledges.
* **Hydrophone.** A research hydrophone array off the lighthouse, cabled to it, records orca calls. Leave it undisturbed.

## Current

Haro Strait's tidal stream runs along the point, setting north-northwest on the flood and south-southeast on the ebb, and the point bends and splits it close inshore.

* **Flood.** Sets north-northwest along the wall, past the lighthouse toward the north cove. Daily peaks at the station run 0.49 to 1.12 m/s over 30 days.
* **Ebb.** Sets south-southeast along the wall, past the cleft and on toward Deadman Bay. Daily peaks at the station run 0.37 to 1.55 m/s over 30 days.
* **Diveable window.** Both slacks serve. Under 0.25 m/s at the station, the slack before flood typically runs 80 to 100 minutes and the slack before ebb 70 to 85 minutes, the shortest about 35 minutes between two strong exchanges. Where a weak exchange falls between two slacks, the current can stay under the threshold for 4 hours or more. A slack followed by a small exchange gives the most forgiving dive.
* **Station timing.** Slack at the point keeps its own timing against the station, arriving well before the predicted slack on some days and well after it on others, and on a weak exchange the point can run an ebb while the station predicts a flood. Look at the water and the kelp before entering to confirm the current has slowed.
* **Close inshore.** At diving depth the current runs parallel to the shore. In the kelp off the point it can run opposite to the main channel, and it can change direction during a dive and differ with depth. On the ebb, the south-setting stream strikes the point, turns away from shore and swirls clockwise in the small bay south of it.
* **Route on slack before flood.** Enter at the cleft and work the wall south of the lighthouse first, so the rising flood carries the return north-northwest to the cleft, or past the lighthouse to the north cove.
* **Route on slack before ebb.** Enter at the north cove, or head north from the cleft first, so the rising ebb carries the return south-southeast to the cleft.

* **Mixed.** South-southeast (SSE), along the shore over more than 30 km, and northwest (NW), along the shore over 12.1 km, each running swell past the entry.
* **Fine.** North-northwest clockwise through southeast (NNW-SE), blocked by the island itself, with 690 m of fetch at most.
* **Wind against current.** West-northwest through northwest (WNW-NW), over 12.1 to 17.0 km, blows against the north-northwest flood set. South-southeast through south (SSE-S), over more than 30 km, blows against the south-southeast ebb set.

## Depth and tide

## Visibility

* **Typical range.** About 3 to 12 m.
* **Season.** Autumn and winter bring the clearest water. Spring and summer plankton blooms and jellies cloud the top 10 m or so, usually with clearer water below.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 7 to 11 °C year round. Drysuit.
* **Surface layer.** Can warm into the low teens °C late in summer.

## Marine life

* **Kelp zone.** Bull kelp and understory kelps over the shallow ledges, with feather boa kelp. Red and green sea urchins graze the stipes, and kelp greenlings, silverspotted and manacled sculpins and hooded nudibranchs live among the fronds.
* **Rock and wall.** Nearly every surface is covered: pink coralline algae, orange cup corals, giant acorn barnacles, chitons, snails and limpets, the orange tentacles of burrowing sea cucumbers in the cracks, solitary glassy sea squirts, funnel and stalked trumpet sponges and sponges of many colours, pink branching hydrocoral, strawberry, crimson, fish-eating and plumose anemones, swimming scallops in numbers, painted sea stars and brittle stars under the rocks.
* **Small life.** Candy stripe shrimp among the crimson anemones, Puget Sound king crab, hermit crabs, Pacific spiny lumpsuckers, and nudibranchs including San Diego dorids.
* **Dens.** Wolf eels under the ledges below the lighthouse and along the small wall to the north, and giant Pacific octopus, often out in the open.
* **Fish.** Black, yellowtail, copper, china, tiger, blue and Puget Sound rockfish, the young of the year schooling over the wall in spring and summer, with lingcod in smaller numbers.
* **Mammals.** Harbor seals and sea lions around the point, and river otters along the shore. Orcas pass close to the lighthouse, most often from May to September.

## Hazards

* **Current.** The stream sweeps the point at up to 1.55 m/s on the largest ebbs at the station, and close inshore it can run against the channel and change with depth. Dive at slack before a small exchange, and confirm the water has slowed before entering.
* **Depth.** The wall drops past 40 m within about 150 m of shore, below the 34 m maximum operating depth of EAN32 at 1.4 bar. Watch depth and no-decompression time closely on the descent.
* **Entry and exit.** The cleft is steep, slick and lined with sharp rock, and the walk to it is long and uphill on the return. Enter only when the swell is small.
* **Wind and wakes.** Wind from south through west-northwest drives waves onto the entry. Large ships in Haro Strait raise short-lived rollers on the rocks.
* **Bull kelp.** The summer canopy can entangle a diver at the surface. Ascend clear of it, and carry a cutting tool.
