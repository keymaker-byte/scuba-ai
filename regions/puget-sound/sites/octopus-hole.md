# Octopus Hole

Octopus Hole is a rock wall in a marine conservation area on the west shore of Hood Canal, beside Highway 101 between Hoodsport and Lilliwaup. A shallow wall about 6 m high runs south parallel to the shore for some 150 m, its crevices holding giant Pacific octopus and wolf eels, and a second, deeper wall lies further out past 20 m. Current is negligible at every stage of the tide, so the dive is planned around visibility, the tide height and the roadside entry. The headline hazards are the highway itself, where the cars park on the shoulder, and the fine silt that blankets the bottom.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Climb over the guard rail at the north end of the lay-by and walk down a few rough rock steps and a short path to a small cobble beach. The steps are steep and uneven with a full set of gear, and many divers carry their gear down in two trips. The beach shrinks to almost nothing at high tide.
* **Parking.** Free. The lay-by on the water side of the road holds about five cars, with more room on the shoulder across the highway. It fills early on a good weekend, and late arrivals park further along the shoulder.
* **Access.** No reservation. Highway 101 drops down to the water here and the trees open out, with signs in the trees marking the conservation area. The site is about 5 km north of Hoodsport and 2 km south of the Lilliwaup bridge. The uplands and tidelands either side of the path are private and posted, so keep to the path and the water. All marine life is protected: the conservation area, from latitude 47.4503 south to 47.4444 and out 183 m from shore, is closed to the taking of every species.
* **Facilities.** None. There is no restroom, staging area or rinse, and divers gear up beside the car on the highway shoulder.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Bearing from the entry.** A compass heading of 145° from the foot of the steps crosses the north end of the shallow wall near its base, a minute or two underwater from shore. Turn south there and follow the wall.
* **Buoy.** A small buoy marks the north end of the wall, and its anchor line leads down to the ledge. Swimming straight to it on the surface and descending the line is the most reliable way onto the structure.
* **Snag.** A dead tree overhangs the water on the small point south of the entry. From the snag, swim straight out from shore about 30 m to reach the north end of the wall.
* **Shallow wall.** Rocky beach gives way to cobble and then silty sand that slopes quickly to a ledge, whose top sits at about 5 to 7 m below MLLW. From the ledge the rock face drops to about 15 m and runs parallel to the shore for some 150 m. The largest crevices, the ones holding octopus and wolf eels, are at the north end; the face further south is smoother, broken by fissures and small boulder shelves, with more delicate life. Past the apparent south end the wall peters out at a large boulder.
* **Deep wall.** Below the shallow wall the bottom drops off quickly again over cobble and mud to a second, smaller wall whose top lies at about 21 m below MLLW, nearly straight out from the entry. Sea whips grow on the cobble around and below it, and the water past it falls away into the deep canal. A bearing from shore easily overshoots it.
* **Getting home.** Follow the base of the shallow wall south, then return north along its top to see what was missed, keeping the wall on the other shoulder. The north end and the buoy lead back to the bearing home, a reciprocal of 325°.

## Current

Octopus Hole is dived independently of the tide. The water here moves at around 0.1 m/s through the whole column, about 0.15 m/s on the largest exchanges, well under any speed that would close the site.

* **Flood.** Sets southwest along the shore, filling the canal toward the Great Bend, and peaks around 0.1 m/s.
* **Ebb.** Sets northeast, draining toward the entrance, at similar strength.
* **Along the wall.** On a large exchange a mild set can run south along the wall, toward its far end, on the ebb as well as on the flood. Read the water at the north end and start the dive into whatever set it finds.
* **Slack.** This reach of the canal behaves as a standing wave, so slack falls near high and low water. Here it runs about an hour ahead of the high and low at Union.
* **Diveable window.** All day, on any exchange.

## Depth and tide

Hood Canal has a large tide here, and it is the biggest control on how the site reads. A high tide adds up to 4 m to every feature, putting the top of the shallow wall at 9 to 11 m and the deep wall near 25 m; a minus tide brings the ledge up to 4 to 5 m. Plan the depth of a deep-wall dive against the tide on the day, since its no-decompression limit on EAN32 shortens quickly as the water rises.

## Hazards

* **Highway parking.** The cars park on the shoulder of Highway 101, and divers gear up and cross beside fast traffic, sometimes across both lanes from the far shoulder. Take extreme care getting in and out of the car and crossing the road. There is nothing to protect a parked car, so leave no valuables in sight.
* **The bank.** The guard rail, the rock steps and the wet rocks at the water's edge are awkward with a full set of gear, worst on the climb back up at the end of the dive.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine silt, and with no current to clear it a fin kick hangs in the water for a long time. Stay neutral and off the bottom, and keep a buddy close in the cloud.
* **Boat traffic.** Small boats run close to this shore. Fly a dive flag, listen on ascent, and come up beside the wall or in the shallows.
* **Depth creep.** The bottom past the shallow wall keeps dropping to the deep wall and beyond into the deep canal. Watch the computer on a bearing out from shore and turn on the no-decompression limit.
* **Lion's mane jellies.** Common in the autumn. Check each other for tentacles before taking off masks and gloves.
* **Low oxygen.** Dissolved oxygen falls in the canal in late summer and autumn. Animals can be found out of their dens or breathing hard, and a wall can look bare below a certain depth.
* **Conservation area.** No taking, feeding or harassing of any marine life.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces east-southeast across Hood Canal, here about 2 km wide, backed by the steep slope of the Olympic foothills and the highway.
* **Bad.** Northeast through east to southeast. Northeast blows down the long reach of the canal with a long fetch and quickly builds chop on the entry; east and southeast are onshore across the canal, short fetch but straight onto the beach. Surface chop can build during a dive, so watch it for the exit.
* **Fine.** West through northwest, offshore and blocked by the high ground behind the site.
* **Mixed.** South and southwest run up the canal roughly parallel to the shore, building a swell that runs past the entry along the shore.
* **Wind against current.** Rarely an issue, since the current is too weak to stack against a wind. Wind alone decides the surface, so read the forecast for the beach.

## Visibility

* **Layers.** A murky surface layer of river runoff and plankton usually sits on top, and visibility often opens up under it, below about 9 to 12 m. Typical visibility on the wall is 3 to 6 m, with clear days past 9 m and bad ones under 1.5 m.
* **Season.** Best from late autumn through winter, when the plankton is down. Spring and summer blooms cut it sharply and can leave a brown layer from the wall base downward. Heavy rain floods the rivers and can drop the top few metres to nothing.
* **Silt.** Divers' fins are the biggest local factor. A busy weekend on the wall stirs up the silt and lowers the visibility for everyone behind.
* **Torch.** Carry one on every dive to see into the crevices and dens.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 8 to 12 °C through the year, coldest in January and February and warmest at the end of summer, around 11 °C in early October. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Hood Canal's surface warms far more than the main Sound in summer, to 15 to 19 °C above a sharp thermocline around 9 m. In winter the surface can be a degree colder than the water at depth.

## Marine life

* **Character.** A conservation area closed to harvesting, and long a site where divers come only to look. The animals in the dens are used to divers.
* **Headliners.** Giant Pacific octopus in dens along the wall, several on a good dive, with females guarding eggs in late winter. Wolf eels in pairs and juveniles, also on eggs in winter. Look for small piles of crab shells outside a hole, which mark an octopus den. Octopus are fewer in winter, when lingcod move onto the wall to spawn and guard eggs.
* **Fish.** Copper, quillback, brown and black rockfish, with occasional vermilion rockfish, schooling around the top of the wall. Lingcod, kelp and painted greenlings, grunt, sailfin and great sculpins, pile perch, striped seaperch and shiner perch in schools, and black-eyed and bay gobies in great numbers along the wall base. Ratfish and flounder on the mud, dogfish, and the occasional sixgill shark below the deep wall.
* **Crustaceans.** Squat lobsters (galatheid crabs) by the hundreds in the cracks. Dungeness crabs in the silt above the wall, decorator, spider, kelp and hermit crabs, and the occasional hairy lithode crab. Spot prawns and other shrimp cover the bottom in summer.
* **Wall life.** White plumose anemones capping the rocks, tube and swimming anemones, orange encrusting sponge, large old yellow sponges, rock scallops and California, orange and burrowing sea cucumbers.
* **Nudibranchs.** Sea lemons, shaggy mouse, red dirona, yellow-rimmed and Dendronotus nudibranchs, and California berthella.
* **Sea stars.** Sunflower stars recovering in numbers, with leather, mottled, blood, long ray, fish-eating and velcro stars.
* **Deep wall.** Sea whips on the cobble around and below the deep wall.
* **Above water.** Bigg's killer whales occasionally pass along the canal.
