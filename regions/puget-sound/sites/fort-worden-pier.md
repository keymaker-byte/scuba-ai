# Fort Worden Pier

A shore dive at the Port Townsend Marine Science Center pier, inside Fort Worden State Park on Admiralty Inlet near the entrance to Port Townsend Bay. White sand slopes gently from the beach out under the pier, littered with broken shells and bottom kelp, then deepens into silty flats beyond it. Old pilings and an artificial reef of sunken tires and concrete cylinders sit alongside the pier, thick with rockfish, greenling, crab and resident wolf eels, with dogfish cruising the sandy edge and octopus denning in the structure. The pier itself, and the current running past it, are the whole dive: swim the pilings at slack and the site delivers; get caught out on a big exchange and the shallow sand under the pier turns into moving water fast.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the sandy beach beside the Marine Science Center pier and boat ramp. Walk in from the parking area, swim out along a piling and follow it down.
* **Parking.** A lot across the road from the pier, at the Marine Science Center. A Discover Pass is required on any vehicle parked in the park, sold as a one day or annual pass at the park office or a self serve kiosk near the beach.
* **Access.** Park hours run 6:30 a.m. to dusk from April 1 to October 15, and 8 a.m. to dusk the rest of the year.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms and a cold shower for rinsing gear behind the Cable House, across the road from the pier. Picnic tables and a snack stand nearby. Camping in the park year round. Air fills in Port Townsend, Port Hadlock and Port Angeles.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The pilings.** Vertical wood pilings run the length of the pier. Swim out along the south side to where they wrap in underneath the deck near the end, and follow them down and back; in low light beneath the deck they carry white plumose anemones and large purple tube worms, a sharply different community from the sunnier pilings on either side.
* **The tire and rubble reef.** North of the pier, an artificial reef of old tires and hollow concrete cylinders sits on the sand, riddled with holes that shelter rockfish and octopus. Descend near the south end of the reef and work north, away from the boat launch.
* **Boat launch.** On the north side of the pier, closed to swimming and diving. Keep clear and stay south of the pier line.
* **Sand to mud.** The bottom is bright sand close to the pier, changing to silt and mud further out and deeper, where it gradually falls away beyond the reef.

## Current

|---|---|
| **Governing station** | Point Wilson (PUG1623), about 2 km northeast |
| **Recommended bin** | bin 45, at 5.2 m |
| **Time offset** | About 60 minutes early on both turns |
| **Flood axis** | South southwest, 204° |
| **Ebb axis** | North northeast, 24° |

* **Flood.** Sets south southwest, parallel to the shore, running from Point Wilson down past the pier.
* **Ebb.** Sets north northeast, back out toward Point Wilson and the Strait.
* **Nearshore push.** Right along the beach, a current sets south toward the pier on both the flood and the ebb, not just the flood; it is weak but persistent, and divers drifting with it can use the pier itself for shelter.
* **Diveable window.** Current stays weak most of the day, rarely above 0.3 to 0.4 m/s even off slack, and the window around each slack runs well over two hours on an average exchange. It narrows and strengthens on a large exchange, and that is when timing the dive to slack matters.

## Depth and tide

Admiralty Inlet's tide swings a few metres a day here, enough to move the pier's working depth by a body length or more between a low and a high.

The pier end and reef, at about 6.5 m below MLLW datum, read about 5.8 m below the surface near a low around −0.6 m, and about 9.2 m near a high around +2.7 m. The outer sand at the dive coordinate, about 10.7 m below datum, reads about 10.0 m near the same low and about 13.4 m near the same high.

## Hazards

* **Current.** Weak most days but the reason to dive near slack, especially on a large exchange; watch for the persistent southward push along the shore even away from the main tidal cycle.
* **Small boats.** The boat launch sits immediately north of the pier, and the site is close enough to shore to see occasional traffic. Listen for engines, and stay down until a boat passes overhead.
* **Fishing line.** Collects on the pilings, especially near the surface and the pier deck. Carry a knife.
* **Shallow depth.** Much of the dive sits in only 6 to 7 m of water; weight to stay down comfortably rather than bobbing near the surface among the pilings.
* **Old creosote pilings.** The pier structure is decades old and due for eventual replacement; the timber pilings are treated with creosote, which leaches into the water as it warms.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces northwest, out across the mouth of Admiralty Inlet toward Point Wilson and the Strait of Juan de Fuca.
* **Bad.** North through west, with open fetch up the inlet and in off the Strait.
* **Fine.** South through east, offshore over the park and the peninsula.
* **Wind against current.** A north or northwest wind blowing against the ebb, which sets north northeast out toward the Strait, stacks chop right on the exposed part of the entry.

## Visibility

* **Best on the flood.** Incoming water from Admiralty Inlet is the cleanest the site sees, particularly toward the end of the flood.
* **Blooms cut it hard.** Like the rest of the Sound, spring and summer plankton blooms are the main thing that degrades visibility here.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 7 to 11 C through the year, recorded around 11 to 12 C in late July. Standard drysuit protocol.

## Marine life

* **Under the pier deck.** White plumose anemones and large purple tube worms cover the pilings in the low light beneath the deck, a noticeably different community from the sunnier pilings either side.
* **Reef and structure.** Rockfish, kelp and painted greenling, white spotted greenling, sculpins including sailfin sculpin, blackeye gobies, flounder, and both giant Pacific and red octopus denning in the tires and hollow concrete cylinders.
* **Wolf eels.** Resident, with juveniles seen regularly in the open along the reef and pilings.
* **Dogfish.** Pacific spiny dogfish lie on the sand at the edge of the pier's shade and cruise past divers in small numbers.
* **Invertebrates.** Sea lemons and other nudibranchs, decorated warbonnets, decorator crabs, hermit crabs, dahlia anemones, giant acorn barnacles, yellow sponges, basket stars, sunflower and other sea stars, orange and white sea cucumbers, gumboot chitons, and shrimp.
* **Open water.** Schools of shiner perch and tube snouts move through the pilings, and harbor seals are seen around the site.
