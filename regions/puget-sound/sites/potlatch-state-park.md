# Potlatch State Park

Potlatch State Park is a shore dive off a long cobble and eelgrass beach on the west shore of southern Hood Canal, at the Great Bend south of Hoodsport. A broad shallow flat runs out from the beach to a line of mooring buoys marking a drop-off, and below it a steep, open silt slope falls to about 46 m, broken only by scattered rock piles and debris. It is a site for animals on open bottom, best known for an unusually strong population of sunflower stars on the upper slope. The current is negligible at every stage of the tide; the headline hazards are boats using the mooring buoys and a slope that keeps dropping well past recreational depth.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Walk from the lot across the lawn and picnic area to the beach and wade in anywhere along it, then surface swim east toward the mooring buoys. The entry is an easy, flat wade over cobble and eelgrass. On a minus tide the flat dries far out and the walk to swimming depth becomes a long trudge over soft bottom in full gear, both ways.
* **Parking.** A large paved lot in the day-use area, on the east side of Highway 101, beside the restrooms. Park at the south end of the lot, near the restrooms. A Discover Pass is required: $10 a day or $45 a year.
* **Access.** The day-use area is open 8 a.m. to dusk year round. The park lies on Highway 101 about 5 km south of Hoodsport; turn east into the lower park. The campground is across the highway on the west side. Clam and oyster harvesting, crabbing and fishing all require a state permit.
* **Facilities.** A restroom building at the south end of the day-use area with cold rinse showers on its back wall, picnic tables, a reservable picnic shelter and a large lawn. The day-use restroom itself is closed for repairs, with portable toilets in its place.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The flat.** From the beach a shallow cobble and silty sand flat runs out about 130 m, at 0 to 3 m below MLLW, with eelgrass over much of it. Cobble along the beach hides small crabs and shrimp, and the eelgrass carries nudibranchs.
* **Buoy line.** A row of five public mooring buoys lies along the edge of the flat and marks the top of the drop-off, at about 4.5 to 6 m below MLLW, some 140 m out from the entry. Swim to the buoys on the surface and descend there.
* **The slope.** Below the buoy line the silty bottom steepens sharply, reaching 18.1 m below MLLW at the site coordinate, 27 m some 35 m further out and 40 m some 145 m past the buoys. It runs as an open slope with only scattered rock piles, old crab traps and debris on it.
* **Sea whip field.** Sea whips grow on the deep slope from about 20 to 37 m below MLLW, thinned by the low-oxygen event of 2025.
* **Foot of the slope.** At about 46 m below MLLW, some 320 m from the entry, the bottom flattens into the deep basin of the canal.
* **Getting home.** The slope runs north and south along the shore, so the way home is always upslope. Come back up to the drop-off, then follow 274°, the reciprocal of the 94° swim out, across the flat to the beach, or make the ascent away from the buoys and swim in on the surface.

## Current

Potlatch is dived independently of the tide. The water here moves at 0.02 m/s or less on every exchange, far under any speed that would close the site.

* **Flood.** Sets southeast toward the Great Bend, peaking at 0.01 to 0.02 m/s.
* **Ebb.** Sets northwest toward the canal entrance, peaking at 0.01 to 0.02 m/s.
* **Slack.** This reach of the canal behaves as a standing wave, so slack falls near high and low water, about 50 minutes after each high and low at Union.
* **Diveable window.** All day on either slack. Peak current stays far under 0.25 m/s on every exchange, so nothing narrows it.
* **Drift.** A barely noticeable drift can run north along the slope below the drop-off.

## Wind

|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | east, 92° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | east, 93° |

The beach is a straight east-facing shore. The water opens east across the canal toward Union, and the long reach of the canal opens up to the north-northeast.

* **Bad.** East (E), onshore over 8.1 km across the canal from Union, builds chop straight onto the beach and the surface swim to the buoys.
* **Short fetch.** Northeast through east-northeast (NE-ENE) and east-southeast through south-southeast (ESE-SSE), onshore over 2.1 to 4.1 km across the canal, short fetch but straight onto the beach.
* **Mixed.** North-northeast (NNE), over 14.6 km, blows up the length of the canal nearly parallel to the shore and runs a swell along the beach past the entry.
* **Fine.** South clockwise through north (S-N): offshore from southwest through north-northwest, blocked by the high ground behind the park and the highway, and cross-shore over 1.0 to 1.3 km from south, south-southwest and north.
* **Wind against current.** The current is too weak to stack against a wind. Wind alone decides the surface, so read the forecast for the beach.

## Depth and tide

Hood Canal carries one of the largest tides in the region, and it decides both how far out the water starts and how deep every feature reads. A big high puts more than 4 m over the flat and moves the top of the drop-off to 8.8 to 10.3 m; a minus tide dries much of the flat and brings the buoy line up to 3.1 to 4.6 m.

## Visibility

* **Layers.** The water is strongly layered. A fresh surface layer from river runoff often holds 1 to 3 m of visibility in the shallows, with a band of suspended plankton and organic detritus below it, usually at 6 to 12 m. Visibility usually opens up beneath that band to 4.5 to 9 m on the slope, then turns silty and milky again toward the foot of the slope.
* **Season.** Best from late autumn through winter, when 6 to 9 m on the slope is common. Spring and summer plankton blooms thicken the mid-water band and can hold it from the shallows down past the drop-off, and heavy rain floods the surface layer with runoff.
* **Tide.** A minus tide leaves the shallows thick and muddy over the exposed flat; a higher tide gives cleaner water over the eelgrass.
* **Silt.** The slope is fine silt, and a careless fin kick hangs in the still water for a long time.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 8 to 11 °C through the year, coldest in late winter. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** Sharply different from depth. In summer the fresh surface layer warms to 15 to 18 °C above a thermocline at about 6 m; in winter it chills to about 7 °C, colder than the water below it.

## Marine life

* **Sunflower stars.** The site's headliner. Healthy adult sunflower stars, many 30 to 60 cm across, gather in numbers on the upper slope from just below the top of the drop-off down to about 20 m, with juveniles among them. Larger ones also range down the deep slope.
* **Deep slope.** Sea whips, with spot prawns, giant sea cucumbers in piles, red octopus in the detritus and occasional Pacific spiny dogfish. The sea whip field has shrunk in recent years, and low oxygen in late 2025 killed much of it.
* **Sand and silt.** Tube-dwelling anemones in abundance, large orange sea pens, giant plumose anemones on rock piles and debris, moon snails and their egg collars, hermit crabs of many kinds, squat lobsters, brittle stars and mottled and other sea stars.
* **Crabs.** Dungeness, red rock, graceful and northern kelp crabs, often many young ones through winter.
* **Fish.** Flatfish everywhere: starry flounder, speckled sanddabs, rock sole and other left- and right-eyed species. Grunt and roughback sculpins, red Irish lords, bay gobies, slender cockscombs, crescent gunnels and bay pipefish in the eelgrass.
* **Octopus.** Red octopus throughout the slope; giant Pacific octopus less often.
* **Squid.** Market squid lay egg masses on the slope in winter and early spring.
* **Eelgrass.** Hermissenda and other nudibranchs, Melibe, and small shrimp and crabs in the cobble.
* **Jellies.** From late winter through spring the water fills with small jellies: tailed, cross, aggregating, water, thimble and fried egg jellies, sea gooseberries and lobed comb jellies, with sea angels at times.
* **Visitors.** Sea lions around the slope at times; seals and otters along the shore.

## Hazards

* **Boat traffic.** Boats use the five public mooring buoys that mark the drop-off, and small boats and personal watercraft run along the shore. Fly a dive flag, listen on ascent, and come up away from the buoys or launch a surface marker before surfacing from the slope.
* **Depth creep.** The slope is steep and featureless, and it keeps dropping to about 46 m with nothing to stop a descent. Watch the computer and turn on the no-decompression limit, especially on a high tide, which adds up to 4.3 m to every depth. On EAN32, stay above 33 m.
* **Minus tides.** A big low dries much of the flat, leaving a long walk out and back over soft bottom in full gear, with muddy, poor visibility over the shallows.
* **Low oxygen.** Southern Hood Canal loses its oxygen at depth in late summer and autumn, in some years down to nothing near the bottom. Animals leave their dens or crowd the shallows, the deep slope can be littered with dead animals and bacterial mats, and fish kills wash up on the beaches. Leave stressed animals alone.
* **Silt and layers.** Silt, suspended detritus and the bloom band can drop visibility to near nothing for a stretch of the descent. Stay close to a buddy through it and carry a torch.
* **Old crab traps.** Abandoned crab traps and their lines lie on the slope. Keep clear of any line on the bottom and carry a cutting tool.
