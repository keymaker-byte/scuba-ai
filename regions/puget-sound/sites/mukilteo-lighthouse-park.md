# Mukilteo Lighthouse Park (Clay Wall)

Shore dive on the Mukilteo waterfront, on the shoreline south of Elliott Point in Possession Sound. A sandy cobble shelf breaks into a long, current sensitive slope of low clay banks, honeycombed with holes that hold wolf eels and giant Pacific octopus. Washington State Parks deeded the lighthouse to the City of Mukilteo in 2001 and the surrounding parkland in 2003, so this is a city park, not a state park; though many divers still call it Mukilteo State Park out of habit. Its headline hazard is current: comfortable on slack or a small exchange, it can run hard enough on a bigger one to be dangerous.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the sandy beach at the south end of the park, near the restrooms and the traffic circle. Enter directly off the beach and head straight out; no boat needed.
* **Parking.** Free, in the lot at the south end of the park near the traffic circle. Fills on summer weekends.
* **Access.** Park hours run 04:00 to 22:00 May through September and 06:00 to 22:00 October through April. Reached off Highway 525 (the Mukilteo Speedway) via Front Street, past the ferry terminal and the lighthouse, to the park at the road's end.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms, picnic tables, six first come first served fire pits along the beach, a playground and a public boat launch at the north end of the park.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Entry line.** Descend from the beach in front of the restrooms and follow the slope straight out.
* **The shelf break.** The sandy cobble shelf holds shallow before the bottom angles down more steeply at about 6 to 7 m below datum.
* **The clay banks.** Low clay banks, 0.3 to 3 m high and pocked with holes, cut across the slope in a band running roughly 12 to 22 m below datum. A shallower bank lies further north along the same shelf, around 7 to 8 m.
* **Past the banks.** Below the lowest bank the slope eases and keeps going into water well past diving depth; there is nothing to follow it out for.

* **Flood.** Sets north, up the shore toward Elliott Point and the ferry terminal.
* **Ebb.** Sets south, away from the point.
* **Diveable window.** On a small exchange the current stays weak most of the day. On a bigger one it runs hard, with a down current reported near the point, and the site is only comfortable within a tight slack window.

## Hazards

* **Boat launch.** A public launch sits at the north end of the park. Dive south of it, fly a marker buoy, and listen for motor noise before any ascent.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine and stirs easily. Keep fins clear of it, both for your own visibility and for the diver behind you.
* **Current on a big exchange.** A larger exchange can run hard here, including a down current reported near the point. Dive only on slack or a small exchange.
* **Small boat traffic.** Working boat launch water in season.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces northwest across Possession Sound toward Whidbey Island.
* **Bad.** West through north, with open fetch across the sound.
* **Fine.** South through east, blowing off the bluff behind the park.
* **Wind against current.** A west or northwest wind through a strong ebb is the combination to avoid.

## Visibility

* **Best on the flood.** As at the T-Dock site immediately north, the flood brings in cleaner water from Admiralty Inlet, so an early flood dive is usually the clearer choice.
* **Silt sensitive.** The fine bottom clouds easily if worked carelessly, and the effect lingers for whoever dives behind you.
* **Blooms.** Spring and summer plankton blooms are the main seasonal spoiler, same as the rest of inner Possession Sound.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 7 to 14 °C through the year, around 12 °C in summer. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Warms well above the depth figure in summer, into the mid teens.

## Marine life

* **Pinnipeds.** Harbor seals work the site year round, and sea lions haul through in winter, sometimes surfacing close enough to startle a diver with a snort before diving on.
* **Clay banks.** Their holes are the site's signature den space, holding juvenile and adult wolf eels and giant Pacific octopus.
* **Fish.** Lingcod and cabezon lie camouflaged on the banks; kelp and painted greenling and an assortment of sculpins, including grunt sculpin, work the structure; gobies and blennies tuck into the smaller holes.
* **Sand and shallows.** Flounder and speckled sanddabs glide over the sandy bottom below the shelf break; green and purple shore crabs and gunnels work the inshore sandy cobble.
* **Invertebrates.** Moon snails, sea anemones, sea stars, piddock clams, sea squirts, hermit crabs, heart crabs and kelp crabs are through the site, with frosted nudibranchs turning up on the banks.
