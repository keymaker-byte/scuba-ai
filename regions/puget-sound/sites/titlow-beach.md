# Titlow Beach

Shore dive at Titlow Park in Tacoma, on the east shore of the Tacoma Narrows south of the Narrows Bridge, where two converging rows of pilings from the old ferry slip stand straight out from the beach, so thickly covered in white plumose anemones that divers call them the Cathedral. A couple of hundred metres south, low sandstone and clay shelves hold octopus and wolf eel dens. The site is shallow and easy to reach, and the pilings mark the south edge of a marine preserve. Its headline hazard is the current: the main Narrows flow runs hard just offshore, and a back eddy sets the opposite way between the pilings and the beach.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Cross the railroad tracks from the parking area to the deck at the water's edge, where picnic tables make a gear-up area. Walk down the ramp beside the deck to the beach and swim straight out toward the pilings, about 90 m. At low tide the beach is cobble and rocky underfoot. The pilings stand directly in front of the deck, and the descent can start among them.
* **Parking.** Free, in the small lot at the foot of 6th Avenue beside the tracks, with more parking in the main park on the other side of the tracks. Spaces are limited and fill on warm weekends. Spaces signed for restaurant customers are reserved for them. Gear can be dropped near the beach before parking.
* **Access.** From State Route 16, take the Jackson Avenue exit just east of the Narrows Bridge, follow Jackson Avenue south to 6th Avenue, and turn right down the hill to the water. Parks Tacoma runs the park, open from half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset. The route to the beach crosses an active BNSF main line with frequent trains; look both ways and cross quickly with gear. The water from the old ferry dock north to the Tacoma Outboard Association leasehold at Hidden Beach is the Titlow Beach Marine Preserve, closed to the taking of shellfish, bottomfish and all other species except salmon on artificial lures from shore or a non-motorized boat.
* **Facilities.** A wood deck with picnic tables and benches over the bulkhead at the entry, a cold freshwater shower for rinsing gear beside it, and a marine life sign. Restrooms are in the park across the tracks.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The beach.** Cobble and sand, shelving gently out to the pilings over sand, shell and cobble. Bull kelp grows over the shallows in summer.
* **The Cathedral.** The two converging rows of pilings of the old ferry slip, the temporary link across the Narrows after the first bridge fell in 1940, about 87 m out on a bearing of 299° from the entry. Each side of the slip has a main structural row and an outer support row. The bottom runs about 4.3 m below MLLW at the south end and about 5.2 m at the north end, deepening toward the outer edge. Many pilings in the outer cluster have fallen and lie on the bottom.
* **The barge.** The remains of a wooden barge lie about 15 m west-southwest of the southwest corner of the slip, about 6.4 m below MLLW.
* **The goalposts.** South of the slip, two lone pilings joined by a crossbeam across their tops. Swimming straight out from them leads to the first of the shelves.
* **The building on pilings.** South of the entry, a building stands over the water on pilings, with more pilings in front of it. Rock ledges and small caves lie beyond them.
* **The shelves.** Long, arcing sandstone and clay shelves 0.3 to 1.2 m tall and 15 to 30 m long, about 200 m south-southwest of the pilings, about 6 to 12 m below MLLW. Their undercuts are the octopus and wolf eel dens.
* **The slope.** West of the pilings the bottom flattens to shell and gravel, then drops away into the Narrows, past 25 m about 500 m out.
* **The way back.** The pilings are the reference: from the shelves, follow the shore north to the slip, then swim the bearing back in on 119°.

* **Flood.** In the main channel offshore of the pilings, the flood runs south out of the Narrows toward Day Island and the South Sound, commonly 1.5 to 2.3 m/s at the station. Inside the pilings, between the slip and the beach, a back eddy turns it around, and the water sets north along the shore.
* **Ebb.** Offshore, the ebb runs north toward the Narrows Bridge, slightly weaker than the flood. Inshore of the pilings the water sets south.
* **The water among the pilings.** The slip and the beach sit in sheltered water that runs far slower than the channel, so the inshore water can feel calm while the channel just past the outer pilings is already moving. The eddy along the shore is rarely fully still.
* **Diveable window.** Either slack, on a small exchange. Summer days often bring two slacks close together with only a small exchange between them, long enough for two dives. Time the dive so the slack falls about midway through it, and plan the route so the end of the dive rides the shoreline eddy north toward the pilings and the entry: on a flood, visit the shelves first and come back north along the shore. Past the outer pilings, a flood carries a diver south toward Day Island and an ebb north toward the bridge, so turn back to the slip as soon as the water starts to move.

## Depth and tide

The South Sound's tides are large, and the tide swings the depth here by up to about 5.7 m, so the pilings read from barely 3 m at a big minus tide to almost 10 m at a high. A high tide makes for the better dive among the pilings.

## Hazards

* **Current.** The main Narrows flow runs strongly just past the outer pilings, and the eddy inshore runs opposite to it. A diver who strays outside the slip off slack can be carried south toward Day Island or north toward the bridge. Stay inside the pilings when the water is moving, and turn the dive early.
* **Lagoon culvert.** A culvert about 1.2 m across runs under the tracks between the beach and Titlow Lagoon, and on a rising tide its intake draws the water into a whirlpool strong enough to pull a floating adult through the pipe. Stay well clear of it while wading and swimming along the shore.
* **Boat traffic.** Small boats from the nearby marina pass close to shore. Display a dive flag, listen for engines, and surface among the pilings or close to the beach.
* **Railroad tracks.** An active main line crosses between the parking and the beach.
* **Bull kelp.** Entanglement in the shallows in summer. Carry a cutting tool.
* **Lion's mane jellies.** Common in autumn. Check each other for tentacles before taking off masks and gloves.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces west across the Narrows toward the Gig Harbor peninsula, about 1.5 km away.
* **Bad.** Southerlies and southwesterlies, which come up from the South Sound past Day Island and Fox Island and push chop onto the beach, and strong northerlies funnelled down the Narrows.
* **Fine.** East, offshore off the bluff behind the park.
* **Wind against current.** A southerly against the south-setting flood, or a northerly against the north-setting ebb, stacks a steep, confused chop in the channel. Under the bridge it can break.

## Visibility

* **Character.** Usually 3 to 6 m among the pilings, often less in the shallows. Best from late autumn through early spring, with stretches of 9 m or more, and worst during the spring and summer plankton blooms. Low tide clouds the shallows; a high tide tends to bring clearer water over the pilings.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** About 8 to 9 C in late winter, rising to about 12 to 13 C in late summer and autumn. Drysuit year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms further in summer, and runs colder than depth in winter.

## Marine life

* **On the pilings.** White plumose anemones cover the pilings so thickly that they form white walls, with giant barnacles, sunlight anemones and tube worms. Nudibranchs are the headline: three-lined aeolids, red flabellinas, opalescent nudibranchs, white-lined and golden dironas, cockscomb nudibranchs, sea lemons including noble sea lemons, leopard, Monterey, Heath's and frosted dorids. Red Irish lords, cabezon, painted greenlings, scalyhead and buffalo sculpins, mosshead warbonnets and gunnels shelter among them, and pile, kelp and striped perch and schools of tubesnouts circle the slip.
* **On the shelves.** Giant Pacific octopus and wolf eels in the dens under the ledges, with lingcod, small rockfish, gunnels, buffalo sculpins and gumboot chitons.
* **On the sand and cobble.** Red rock crabs, decorator crabs, kelp crabs, Dungeness crabs, hermit crabs and shrimp, flounder, sea pens, sea cucumbers and ratfish. Old bottles lie scattered over the bottom, and small red octopus live in them; check a bottle for a resident before moving it.
* **Sea stars.** Leather, mottled, sunflower and giant sea stars.
* **Seasonal and passing.** Pacific spiny lumpsuckers in late winter and spring, schools of small baitfish in summer, and harbor seals.
