# Seacrest Cove 3

Shore dive at the north end of Seacrest Park on the West Seattle waterfront, also known as Alki Cove 3, the small cove just north of the water taxi dock with the downtown skyline across Elliott Bay. Most of the bottom is silty sand and sea lettuce falling steeply away to the east-northeast, with old pilings and rock piles at the cove's north end and debris scattered across the slope, often quiet when the park is busy and a rewarding night dive. The headline hazard is the water taxi: its dock bounds the cove on the south side, and a buoy line marks the edge of the dive area along it.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Concrete stairs lead from the Harbor Avenue SW promenade down to the beach just north of the café building and the water taxi dock. The ledge beside the stairs makes a convenient place to gear up. The beach is easy cobble and sand, and the bottom drops quickly once in the water; swim out east-northeast, with the dock and its buoy line to the right.
* **Parking.** Free curbside parking along Harbor Avenue SW, and along the far side of the street. It fills early on weekends, when the walk grows longer.
* **Access.** From I-5, take the West Seattle Bridge to the Harbor Avenue SW exit, turn right, and follow the waterfront about 2 km to Seacrest Park at 1660 Harbor Avenue SW. The King County Water Taxi from Pier 50 downtown lands at the dock beside the cove. The park is open 4:00 to 23:30; night dives finish before closing.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms, a drinking fountain and a seasonal rinse shower at the pier building a short walk south, beside the Marination Ma Kai café, which keeps an AED inside. A hand carry boat launch is at the north end of the park.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **Layout.** The cove runs northwest from the water taxi dock along the beach toward the Don Armeni boat ramp. Facing the water, the dock is on the right and the pilings at the far end of the cove on the left.
* **Buoy line.** A line of buoys marks the edge of the dive area along the water taxi dock, with a rope on the bottom carrying small floats to show it underwater. Stay on the cove side of it. A white diver warning buoy stands off the cove and makes a convenient descent point.
* **Pilings and rock piles.** Old pilings stand at the north end of the cove, visible from the beach, with rock piles around their base. Plumose anemones cover the pilings, and schools of perch circle them; this is the richest part of the site.
* **Debris.** Logs, tires, cinderblocks, old carpet, a traffic cone and many old bottles lie scattered over the sand, a loose junkyard with no single centre. The remains of a small boat lie on the slope seaward of the pilings, and another hull lies between the pilings and the boat ramp.
* **Deep line.** A guide line runs deep on the slope toward the neighbouring cove southeast of the dock. Check its route against the buoy line before following it.
* **Depth as a guide.** The slope is steep and even, so depth reads as distance from shore: about 3 m at 25 m out, 9 m at 50 m, 17 m at 100 m, 29 m at 150 m and 39 m at 200 m below MLLW along the swim out. Up the slope is home in any visibility. Below the shallows the bottom is open sand, with scattered debris its only structure.

## Current

The cove sits deep inside Elliott Bay, away from the main tidal streams, and stays diveable through the whole cycle on any exchange; pick the entry time for tide height, boat traffic and daylight.

* **Flood.** Sets southwest, in toward the beach at an angle. Daily peaks at the station run 0.10 to 0.22 m/s over 30 days, and the flow on the cove's bottom peaks near 0.02 m/s.
* **Ebb.** Sets northeast, out across the bay. Daily peaks at the station run 0.07 to 0.14 m/s on the 28 days of 30 that carry an ebb maximum, and the flow on the cove's bottom peaks near 0.02 m/s.
* **Diveable window.** Any tide. The station stays under 0.25 m/s through every day of the month, springs included. Ship wakes and wind chop move more water at the entry than the tide does.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | east-northeast, 61° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | east-northeast, 58° |

The beach faces east-northeast across Elliott Bay, and the swim out runs straight off it.

* **Short fetch.** North through east-southeast (N-ESE), onshore over 1.9 to 4.6 km across the bay onto the entry, which builds chop on the beach; north has the longest fetch of these, in through the bay mouth.
* **Mixed.** North-northwest (NNW), cross-shore over 5.5 km down the Sound, running chop along the beach.
* **Fine.** Southeast clockwise through northwest (SE-NW): offshore from south through west-northwest, off the land directly behind the beach, and cross-shore over 1.1 km or less from southeast, south-southeast and northwest.
* **Wind against current.** The current is too weak to stack against any wind.

## Visibility

* **Range.** From under 1 m to 9 m or more in winter; 3 to 6 m is usual.
* **Silt.** The bottom is fine silt that clouds at a touch, and the shallows usually read worse than the slope below them. A late dive behind other groups, or the end of a long one, can leave almost nothing to see.
* **Seasons.** Winter runs clearest, before the light returns to feed the plankton; spring and summer blooms bring the haze and the green.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** 8 to 13 °C through the year. Standard drysuit protocol.
* **Surface layer.** Warms to about 15 °C in late summer while the slope stays cooler; dress for the depth.

## Marine life

* **Character.** Most of the life gathers on the pilings, the rock piles and the debris, each a small patch of shelter on open sand and sea lettuce. The site comes alive after dark.
* **Pilings.** Plumose anemones sheath the pilings, grazed by shaggy mouse nudibranchs, and large schools of striped seaperch and shiner perch circle them. Bioluminescence lights up around the pilings on a night dive.
* **Fish.** Painted and kelp greenlings, quillback, brown and juvenile rockfish, buffalo, staghorn and sailfin sculpins, red Irish lords, northern ronquil, blackbelly eelpouts, gunnels, northern clingfish, snailfish, tubesnouts and rock sole, with spotted ratfish over the sand after dark.
* **Shrimp and crabs.** Coonstripe shrimp and spot prawns are common, swarming the rock piles at night, with red rock crabs and kelp crabs.
* **Octopus and squid.** Pacific red octopus turn up across the slope, active after dark. Stubby squid and squid egg clusters appear in winter and spring.
* **Invertebrates.** Orange sea cucumbers, moon snails, diamondback tritonia, frosted and hooded nudibranchs, young sunflower stars, and moon jellies in the water column.
* **Seasons.** Sea lettuce and kelp thin out over winter, and the shallows hold less life until spring.
* **Octopus preserve.** Seacrest Park's three coves are a WDFW octopus protection area, closed to the harvest of octopus.

## Hazards

* **Water taxi.** The passenger ferry runs year round from the dock on the south side of the cove. City code bars diving within 46 m of the Seacrest pier, dock and floats; the buoy line marks the limit. Stay on the cove side of it, and never surface in the boat's approach to the dock.
* **Boat traffic.** The Don Armeni boat ramp lies just northwest of the cove, and kayaks launch from the beach at the north end of the park. Small boats and jet skis work the shoreline, heaviest in summer, and their wakes break on the entry. Fly a dive flag, as the signs on site ask, and listen before any ascent.
* **Bike lane.** A busy bike lane runs along the promenade above the beach. Watch for fast bikes when crossing it with gear, and take extra care crossing Harbor Avenue SW from parking on the far side.
* **The slope.** The bottom keeps dropping on an even slope past 40 m, reaching 39 m below MLLW only 200 m off the beach. Watch the gauge, especially at night or in poor visibility.
* **Silt.** The fine bottom stirs easily; a careless fin near the pilings or the debris fogs the water for everyone behind you.
