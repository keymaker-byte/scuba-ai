# Pinnacle Rock

Pinnacle Rock is a shore dive on the Strait of Juan de Fuca between Sekiu and Neah Bay, named for the tall, slender rock pinnacle, trees growing on its top, that stands in the intertidal area and marks the site from the highway. A row of trees screens the beach from the road; past them the shore opens onto a sweeping view of the strait. Cobble and boulder in the shallows give way to a gently sloping bottom of sand and cobblestone, scattered with large kelp covered rocks that form tunnels and channels a diver can work in either direction from the entry. It is remote, open water with a direct line to the Pacific, and its headline hazard is exactly that: strong current and surge that build quickly away from slack, on a stretch of coast with nothing between it and the open ocean to break either one.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** From the dirt turnout, follow the short path through the band of trees down to the rocky, cobble beach and enter directly off it. The pinnacle itself, visible from the turnout before starting down, confirms the spot. Divers can work the rock and kelp ground in either direction from the entry.
* **Parking.** An informal dirt turnout on the north side of Highway 112 (SR 112), just past mile marker 4 heading west from Sekiu. Space is limited and unmarked; no facilities.
* **Access.** Shore access is available 24 hours a day.
* **Facilities.** None at the site. Sekiu is about 10.5 road miles east, Neah Bay about 6.5 miles west.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The pinnacle.** A tall, slender rock standing in the intertidal area, with trees growing on its top, visible from the highway turnout and the beach; it is the landmark the site is named for and confirms the entry.
* **The beach.** Rocky and cobble, backed by a narrow band of trees that screens it from the road.
* **The bottom.** Cobble and boulder in the shallows give way to a gently sloping sandy, cobblestone bottom carrying large rocks wrapped in kelp, with sandy channels and eelgrass patches between them. The channels are wide enough to work without pushing into the kelp itself.
* **Direction.** Open in either direction along the shore from the entry; there is no single fixed route.
* **Staying found.** The rocks and kelp break the ground into a maze similar to Sekiu Jetty and One Mile Beach further east. Watch compass and depth continuously.

| **Recommended bin** | bin 75, at 12.4 m |
| **Time offset** | About 80 minutes before the station for slack before flood, near the station's own time for slack before ebb |
| **Flood axis** | east-southeast, 115° |
| **Ebb axis** | west-northwest, 290° |

* **Flood.** Sets east-southeast, parallel to shore toward Sekiu and Pillar Point; peak flood at the open-water station commonly runs 0.1 to 0.5 m/s, occasionally higher on a big exchange.
* **Ebb.** Sets west-northwest, parallel to shore toward Neah Bay and the strait's mouth; peak ebb at the station runs a similar 0.1 to 0.8 m/s and tends to run the stronger of the two.
* **Diveable window.** The rock and kelp ground breaks up and shelters most of the flow near slack, but this site sits further out toward the open mouth of the strait than Sekiu Jetty or One Mile Beach, with a longer, more direct exposure, and both current and surge build faster once away from the largest rocks. Dive only in calm conditions, time the entry to slack, and treat any dive that runs into a developing exchange as a drift dive, working with the flow or across it rather than against it.

## Hazards

* **Current and surge.** Open, current swept water with a direct, unbroken line to the Pacific. Both build quickly once away from slack and away from the shelter of the largest rocks. Dive only on a calm day, time the entry to slack, and plan any exchange period dive as a drift dive.
* **Breaking waves at entry.** When waves are breaking along the shoreline, entry and exit are difficult and dangerous. Make a visual check of surface conditions before committing, and be willing to abort if either diver is uncomfortable with the water or the surface conditions.
* **Kelp entanglement.** Long kelp stalks form tunnels and thick tangles, heaviest in summer and fall. Carry a cutting tool and work through it with a buddy.
* **Navigation.** The scattered rocks and kelp break the ground into a maze it is easy to get turned around in.
* **Remoteness.** No facilities at the site and a long drive to help in either direction, typical of this reach of the strait; a problem here is a longer way from care than a dive closer to Sekiu or Port Angeles.

## Wind

* **Orientation.** The beach faces north into the open strait, with a long, unbroken fetch to the Pacific.
* **Bad.** North through northwest.
* **Fine.** South through southeast, off the land.
* **Wind against current.** A northwesterly blowing against the ebb, which sets toward the open strait's mouth, is the combination to avoid.

## Visibility

* **Typical range.** Runs roughly 5 to 9 m in summer and early fall, similar to Sekiu Jetty and One Mile Beach further east, occasionally reaching into the low teens of metres. A swell event or a bloom can drop it under 2 m.
* **Season.** Best conditions tend to run summer into early fall, matching the kelp forest's own peak.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 6 to 9 °C, reflecting the strait's direct oceanic connection. Standard drysuit protocol year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the low teens late in summer.

## Marine life

* **Fish.** Kelp greenlings, lingcod, cabezon and black rockfish work the rocks and kelp canopy.
* **Invertebrates.** Sea urchins, moon snails, sunflower sea stars, ochre sea stars and basket stars are common on the rock and sand.
* **In the water column.** Dense schools of mysid shrimp and amphipods fill the water among the kelp, and sand lance erupt from the sandy channels between the rocks.
* **Marine mammals.** Harbor seals work this stretch of coast, and grey whales are seen here occasionally, including inside the cove itself.
