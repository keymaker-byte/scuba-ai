# One Mile Beach

One Mile Beach is a shore dive on the Strait of Juan de Fuca about 1.6 km west of Sekiu, reached on foot along the old railroad grade above the shore. A gentle, sandy and gravel slope carries a scatter of rocks wrapped in kelp out to a rock and kelp ledge, beyond which the bottom drops away quickly into deeper water. The beach itself is piled with driftwood logs, a reminder of the pounding this open, north facing shoreline takes in winter storms. Its headline hazard is that same exposure: open water with a direct line to the Pacific, where surge and current build fast once away from the shelter of the kelp and rock.

## Getting there

* **Entry.** Park at the Sekiu boat launch, at the Three Sisters rock formation on the north end of town, and follow the old abandoned railroad grade northwest along the hillside above the shore. After about 1.6 km the trail drops down onto the beach; enter directly off the sand and gravel into the shallows and swim out toward the rocks and kelp. Older dive guides describe a shorter route down a separate dirt spur off Highway 112 closer to the site; confirm locally whether that access still exists before relying on it.
* **Parking.** The gravel lot at the Sekiu boat launch, now managed by the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife. Day use only, a Discover Pass or WDFW Vehicle Access Pass is required.
* **Access.** Shore access is available 24 hours a day. Sections of the shoreline further along the coast, toward the sea caves to the east and Eagle Point to the west, are passable only at low tide; the walk to One Mile Beach itself runs above the beach on the old grade and does not depend on the tide.
* **Facilities.** Restrooms at the boat launch. Food, lodging, boat rentals and moorage at Mason's Olson Resort and elsewhere in Sekiu and Clallam Bay.

## Navigation and landmarks

* **The beach.** Sand and gravel, backed by driftwood logs. The trail from the boat launch comes down onto it from the hillside to the south.
* **The rock and kelp ground.** Scattered rock formations wrapped in kelp sit on a sandy, gently sloping bottom, with channels and small tunnels winding between the rocks.
* **The drop-off.** The slope steepens sharply beyond about 20 m below MLLW; stay on the shelf rather than following it down.
* **Staying found.** The rock and kelp cover breaks up the terrain into a maze. Watch compass and depth continuously.

* **Flood.** Sets east-southeast, parallel to shore toward Sekiu, with daily peaks of 0.19 to 0.82 m/s at the open-water station over 30 days.
* **Ebb.** Sets west-northwest, parallel to shore toward the open strait and the Pacific, with daily peaks of 0.35 to 0.84 m/s at the station over the same 30 days.
* **Diveable window.** A typical window under 0.25 m/s at the station runs about 110 to 240 minutes. The rock and kelp ground shelters most of the dive from the tidal flow; current among the rocks is generally a non-factor near slack. It builds quickly once past the drop-off, so stay on the shelf once away from slack, and dive only on a calm day with weak surge.

## Wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Entry shore facing** | north, 8° |
| **Dive area shore facing** | north, 7° |

The beach faces north into the open strait.

* **Bad.** Northwest clockwise through east-northeast (NW-ENE), onshore over 22 km to more than 30 km, straight up the open strait with a long, unbroken fetch to the Pacific, or across it.
* **Mixed.** East (E), cross-shore over more than 30 km down the strait, which runs a swell along the beach past the entry.
* **Fine.** East-southeast clockwise through west-northwest: offshore from southeast through west-southwest, off the land, and cross-shore over 5 km or less from the east-southeast, west and west-northwest.
* **Wind against current.** West-northwest through north-northwest (WNW-NNW) against the ebb, which sets toward the open strait, over 2.5 km to more than 30 km, is the combination to avoid. East through east-southeast (E-ESE) against the flood, over 4.8 km to more than 30 km.

## Visibility

* **Typical range.** Runs roughly 5 to 9 m in summer and early fall, occasionally reaching into the low teens of metres. A swell event or a bloom can drop it under 2 m.
* **Season.** Best conditions tend to run summer into early fall, matching the kelp forest's own peak.

## Temperature

* **At depth.** Roughly 6 to 9 °C, reflecting the strait's direct oceanic connection. Standard drysuit protocol year round.
* **Surface layer.** Warms into the low teens late in summer.

## Marine life

* **Invertebrate cover.** Sea urchins and sea cucumbers work the rocks and sand, alongside morning sun stars, sunflower stars, blood stars and lemon nudibranchs.
* **Fish.** Kelp greenlings, lingcod, rockfish and rock scallops among the rocks, with schools of small fish moving through the kelp.
* **Cephalopods.** Wolf eels and giant Pacific octopuses den in the rocky holes along the ledge.
* **In the water column.** Dense clouds of mysid shrimp and amphipods drift through the kelp.
* **Beyond the rocks.** Harbor seals work the site occasionally.

## Hazards

* **Current and surge.** Open, current swept water with a direct line to the Pacific. Both build quickly beyond the drop-off and once outside the kelp and rock's inshore shelter. Time the dive to slack, dive only in calm conditions, and treat an exchange-period dive as a drift dive planned around the current's direction.
* **Driftwood and surf.** The beach carries an unusually heavy load of storm driven logs. Watch footing on the beach and stay clear of logs moving in the surf during entry and exit.
* **Kelp entanglement.** A thick kelp forest, especially during the growing season. Carry a cutting tool and work through it with a buddy.
* **Navigation.** The scattered rock formations and kelp break the ground into a maze it is easy to get turned around in, and easy to drift shallow while working around the rock piles.
* **Remoteness.** A long walk in from the nearest parking, in a remote part of the strait; a problem here is a longer way from help than a dive closer to town.
